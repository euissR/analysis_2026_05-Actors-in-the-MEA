Embassies1

China has the widest diplomatic network in Africa with embassies in all the 53 countries with which it has diplomatic relations. Thus, the only country in which China does not have an embassy is Eswatini.

The EU has the second largest diplomatic network with Delegations in 50 countries. Comoros, Equatorial Guinea and Seychelles do not have a resident EU Ambassador but are covered by Delegations in neighbouring countries.

The US has embassies in 49 countries,

and Russia and Türkiye in 44.

Algeria, Egypt, Ethiopia, Kenya, Senegal and Tunisia stand out as crowded diplomatic arenas, with 15 of these external actors maintaining an embassy in each country.

High-level visits

In addition to permanent diplomatic representation, high-level visits conducted at the level of heads of state or government or minister for foreign affairs also underline the concentration of diplomatic capital. But they also highlight political investment in the diplomatic relations with countries or regions.

Between January 2019 and July 2026, high-level visits of the actors tracked are strongly concentrated in North Africa, which accounted for 45.7% of all visits.

The EU and Türkiye visited North Africa 27 times each. Both concentrated their visits to Egypt, respectively 15 and 13 times, but the second most visited country in North Africa has been Tunisia for the EU

and Algeria for Türkiye.

The EU visited Africa more than any other actor tracked with 75 visits across 25 African countries. Its leading destinations included Egypt (15), Ethiopia (7), and Kenya, Rwanda and South Africa (5 each). The EU also visited Central Africa and East Africa and the Horn more than other actors, respectively six and 17 times.

Türkiye recorded 54 visits across 24 countries, but half of its visits were to North Africa – particularly Egypt (13), Algeria (7) and Libya (5).

Türkiye was also the actor that visited West Africa more often with 15 visits distributed across 10 West African countries. In this period, it never visited Burkina Faso, Cabo Verde, Cote d'Ivoire and Mauritania.

China made 43 visits across 29 countries, giving it the widest and most geographically balanced coverage.

11 visits were to Southern Africa and the Indian Ocean – of which four to South Africa.

It also visited Senegal and Ghana three times each within overall 10 visits to West Africa.

China also visited East Africa and the Horn 10 times – of which seven visits were to Ethiopia.

Its visits to Central Africa were more evenly distributed with one visit each to Burundi, Chad, DRC, Gabon and Congo.

In North Africa, China conducted only seven visits of which four to Egypt.

India, Japan and South Korea showed a stronger focus on Southern Africa, directing 13 of 27

12 of 30

and four of 10 visits respectively to Southern Africa and the Indian Ocean, especially South Africa and Mauritius.

Among the actors tracked, Japan was the one that visited the Southern Africa and Indian Ocean region more often.

Summits

Diplomatic outreach has also multiplied through summits with the African continent. This shows the increased interest of multiple actors to strengthen relations with African countries politically and economically as many summits are followed by economic or business forums. But it also reveals the imbalance when summits are organised between one country and a continent – the so-called Africa+1 summits.2

The AU–EU summits are the only summits in a continent-to-continent format to which 81 national delegations and several regional institutions on both continents contribute and participate since their launch with Cairo summit in 2000. Since then, the AU–EU summits take place approximately every three years with the last in Luanda, Angola, in 2025.

Some EU Member states also have summits with Africa, notably France since 1973 and more recently Italy since 2024.

However, the most common format for Summit diplomacy with Africa is the so-called Africa+1 format. And it is becoming a crowded arena, even if their regularity varies.

Established frameworks with the EU, China and Japan have proved relatively reliable and recurrent.

The Forum on China–Africa Cooperation (FOCAC), also launched in 2000, has been relatively stable with the last summit hosted in Beijing in 2024 and the next one scheduled in the Republic of Congo in 2027.

Launched in 1993, the Tokyo International Conference on African Development (TICAD) also takes place every three years with the last summit hold in 2025 and the next scheduled for 2028.

On the contrary, newer initiatives by Russia, Türkiye, Saudi Arabia and South Korea have been more intermittent.

Russia hold the first summit with Africa in 2019, the second in 2023, and the third in October 2026 (TBC).

Saudi Arabia only convened one summit with Africa in 2023 and South Korea in 2024.

To the list of actors reaching out to Africa, Iran has focused more on economic diplomacy since 2023 when it launched the Iran-Africa Economic Cooperation Summit. Initially almost exclusively dedicated to West African countries, it has expanded to other African regions in its last two editions in 2024 and 2025.

Also, Israel proposed the launch of a summit with Africa in 2017, but it was initially postponed and de facto never held.

Partnerships

Embassies, visits and summits, however, do not automatically translate into partnerships of equal depth or type. Several external actors categorise their international partnerships differently, from consistent formalisation of all partnership as in the case of China to more selective or sectoral partnerships. The level, depth and the relative strategic importance of sectoral cooperation obviously indicate the trend of the strategic relevance of the partnership itself.

China stands out for the breadth of its formalised partnerships, although these vary in depth. At the latest FOCAC summit in 2024, China confirmed it had strategic partnerships with all the 53 countries with whom it has diplomatic relations and upgraded several of ones already existing.3

Other actors formalise partnerships more selectively both from the perspective of geographic distribution and type.

The EU has 11 partnerships with African countries, of which four strategic partnerships with Egypt, Nigeria, South Africa and Tunisia, four strategic partnerships on critical raw materials (CRMs) with DRC, Rwanda, South Africa and Zambia, one strategic partnership on green hydrogen and CRM value chains with Namibia, and a green partnership with Morocco. In 2026, the EU and Ghana also formalised a security and defence partnership.

The US identifies four African countries as major non-NATO allies (Morocco, Tunisia, Egypt and Kenya) out of seven identified partnerships in Africa.

The UAE focuses its partnerships with African countries on the economy through seven comprehensive economic partnership agreements (Angola, Central African Republic, Democratic Republic of Congo, Republic of Congo, Gabon, Kenya and Mauritius).

India labels four partnerships as strategic and one with Mauritius as enhanced strategic partnership. Russia and Türkiye four each.

Russia labels its partnerships with Algeria, Egypt and South Africa as strategic.

The DRC, Egypt, Morocco, Nigeria and South Africa have the highest concentration of partnerships with the external actors considered.

Voting at the UN General Assembly

The extent and depth of partnerships does not automatically translate into alignment. On the contrary, with their 54 votes at the UN General Assembly, African states express significant agency in their voting patterns.

Abstention is by far the predominant position among African states, reflecting a general tendency towards non-alignment.

However, on average African countries abstained slightly less in the period 2022–2026 (46%) than in 2016–2021 (51%).

However, on average African states align more frequently with the EU than with Russia on Ukraine.

African countries aligning with the EU more than 50% of times include Cabo Verde, Liberia, and Seychelles.

Those aligning more frequently with Russia include Eritrea, Sudan, Burundi, Zimbabwe and Mali.

However, while Burundi’s alignment with Russia decreased after 2022, Mali’s alignment with Russia increased.

Africa aligns more often with the EU also on other issues such as the territories occupied by Israel and Palestinian territories*.

Between 2016 and 2025, African countries abstained on average in 13% of UN General Assembly votes on resolutions concerning the Israeli-occupied territories and did not vote in 23%.4

On average, African countries aligned more often with the EU, with the alignment rate increasing from 54% between 2016 and 2022 to 59% between 2023 and 2025.

Since 2023, African countries have abstained in 6% of UN General Assembly votes on Gaza and the Palestinian territories* and did not vote in 18%.

Alignment with Israel has been close to zero, while alignment with the EU has averaged 78%.

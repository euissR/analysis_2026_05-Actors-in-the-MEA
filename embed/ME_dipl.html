Diplomatic network

Türkiye has the broadest diplomatic footprint across the region.

Israel has by far the narrowest regional network.

The EU has Delegations in 9 countries and an office in the Palestinian territories*, with a new Delegation planned in Oman.

Russia, China and India have 11 diplomatic representations and one office in the Palestinian territories* each.

High-level visits

Beyond the presence of diplomatic missions, frequent high-level visits also reflect the nature of relations between countries and the willingness to invest political capital in the relation. Between 2019 and July 2026, the US (110), Türkiye (94), Iran (81) and the EU (61) accounted for most high-level visits of external actors(2) to the Middle East and the Gulf. The concentration of visits reveals different patterns of diplomatic outreach.

US visits focused on Israel and Saudi Arabia. Türkiye’s visits focused on Qatar and Saudi Arabia. Iran’s visits focused on Qatar and Iraq. The EU’s visits focused on Jordan and the UAE.

However, intra-regional visits also registered frequent travelling of heads of state or government or foreign ministers. GCC countries visited each other 265 times. Qatar and Kuwait were the most frequent visitors, especially to Saudi Arabia, while Bahrain visited the UAE more often and Saudi Arabia was the second more frequent destination.

Visits among GCC countries intensified in 2024–2025. Around the same period, in 2025, Iran also increased its visits to the region, particularly Oman, Qatar and Saudi Arabia. In this case, intensification of diplomatic contacts clearly corresponded to a period of increased insecurity in the region.

Partnerships(3)

Stronger bilateral relations are also reflected in how external actors describe their partnerships with regional actors. Several external actors have developed formal partnerships with countries in the Middle East and the Gulf. However, the breadth and depth of formal partnerships varies as does their geographical distribution.

China has comprehensive strategic partnerships with Bahrain, Saudi Arabia and the UAE, and an innovative comprehensive partnership with Israel. Less developed are the formal partnerships with Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Oman, Palestine*, Syria and Qatar that are defined as strategic.

Most countries in the region are US major non-NATO allies (Bahrain, Egypt, Israel, Jordan, Kuwait, Morocco, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia). Israel though is also a strategic partner for the US.

Türkiye has formal partnerships with Qatar, Iraq, Jordan and the UAE, with its Supreme Strategic Committee with Qatar representing the highest level.

India has a comprehensive strategic partnership with the UAE and strategic partnerships with Israel, Kuwait, Oman and Saudi Arabia.

Russia has a strategic partnership with the UAE and strategic cooperation with Saudi Arabia.

The EU has Strategic and Comprehensive Partnerships with Jordan and a strategic partnership with the GCC. It is also negotiating individual strategic partnerships with GCC countries: negotiations with Qatar and the UAE were launched in December 2025, followed by Bahrain in July 2026.

Summits

Dedicated summits increasingly signal political eagerness to strengthen partnerships.

The EU and the US have held summits with the GCC countries since 2024 and 2015, respectively. A second EU-GCC summit is scheduled in 2026.

China launched its summit with the Arab States in 2022, a second summit initially scheduled for 2026 has been postponed sine die. Russia sought to organise a summit with the Arab states, but it was postponed indefinitely.

Voting at the UN General Assembly

The intensity and depth of diplomatic relations are also often reflected in voting patterns at the UN General Assembly. Abstentionism on UNGA resolutions pertaining Ukraine is quite widespread in the region. At the same time, among Gulf countries it decreased from 90% (2016-2021) to 68% (2022-2026), while in the Middle East it increased from 45% to 49%.

Israel is the country that abstains less and that more consistently vote against Russian positions. Until 2024, Syria consistently voted with Russia; following the fall of the Assad regime, it either abstained or was absent. In the 2016-2022 period, Iran aligned with Russia at every vote, but alignment rate dropped to 53% after 2022 as its abstention rate reached 40%. China and India aligned with Russia 90% and 60% of times respectively prior to 2022.

Their abstention rate also increased significantly after 2022, reaching 60% for China and 100% for India.

The US voted with Russia only once, in February 2025, while EU Member States, Japan and Korea consistently voted against Russia’s position.

On the contrary, abstentionism in the region is very limited for UNGA resolutions on Israeli occupied territories and Palestine*.

Countries in the Middle East and the Gulf consistently voted against Israel’s position on UNGA resolutions on Israeli occupied territories, including the Golan Heights and East Jerusalem over the past ten years. On the contrary, the US aligned with Israel in 93% of votes, increasing average alignment from 86% to 100% after 2023.

Overall, states in the Gulf and the Middle East – with the exceptions of Israel – align more often with the EU member states. After 2023, alignment increased from about 46% to about 56%.

EU member states abstained on average 50% of times prior to 2023 and 36% after, while alignment with Israel increased to 3% after 2023.

On Gaza-related UNGA resolutions since 2023, the US aligned with Israel in 92% of votes, while EU Member States only partially aligned with Israel and frequently abstained.

Countries in the Gulf and the Middle East align with the EU average vote in about 83% of cases. On the contrary, Israel and the US align with the EU in 5% of votes.

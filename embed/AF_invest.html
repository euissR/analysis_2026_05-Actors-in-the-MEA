Foreign direct investment

Total recorded inward FDI stock in Africa was broadly unchanged, at $938 bn in 2019–21 and $948 bn in 2022–24(3). The EU remained the largest recorded investor, but its share fell from 30.8% to 28.5%, as its stock declined from $289 bn to $270 bn(4).

Gulf investors gained ground: their recorded stock rose by about 40%, from $38 bn to $54 bn (4.1% to 5.7% of the total), mostly through UAE positions in Mozambique and Mauritius. India’s share rose by a similar margin, from 3.4% to 5.2%, almost entirely through Mauritius.

The shares of the US (11.2% and 11.5%) and China (4.0% and 4.3%) were broadly stable.

In roughly one in three African economies, a single tracked external actor accounts for more than half of recorded inward FDI stock*. On 2022–24 averages, this is true in 18 of 52 economies: the EU accounts for 12 of these relationships, China for five, and Türkiye for one (Somalia, where recorded stock averages only $11 mn).

The EU combined scale with leading position in larger economies – it was the leading listed counterpart in 35 economies representing 86% of measured African GDP. China was present in almost as many economies but led in only 13, which together accounted for 5.5% of Africa's recorded inward FDI stock and around 12% of measured GDP; many of them are resource-oriented economies.

Its importance therefore lies less in its continental investment share than in its positions within particular hosts.

The Gulf’s footprint was more concentrated; Mozambique, Mauritius and Morocco held 84% of its recorded African position, and the UAE accounted for over four fifths of it.

The US, meanwhile, held substantial positions without often being the leading counterpart: it accounted for 22% of Egypt’s recorded inward stock and 29% of Algeria’s.

Official development finance

Among the bilateral actors and blocs tracked, Europe (MS plus EU institutions) remained the largest source of official finance to Africa, but its lead narrowed. Its net ODA fell by 18.3%, to a total of $58.6 billion in 2022–24, with most of the decline coming from EU Institutions. By contrast, US net ODA rose by 11.0% to a total of $45.4 billion, narrowing the gap with Europe. Saudi Arabia ranked third on net ODA among the tracked actors.

Their broader official-finance portfolios, including OOF (other financial flows), also differed: Europe and the US remained predominantly grant-based, particularily the latter, while loans accounted for 63.2% of Japan’s gross official finance*. Saudi Arabia’s reported ODA disbursements were also largely grant-based.

China operated through a fundamentally different model**. Its $47.2 billion in adjusted 2019–23 commitments was overwhelmingly OOF-like rather than ODA and two-thirds was infrastructure-related, and its engagement was geographically concentrated.

Geographic concentration also differs markedly across actors. Europe had the broadest footprint, while the US was more concentrated in East Africa and the Horn and China in West Africa. The UAE was more concentrated still: around half of its country-coded* African finance went to Central Africa, with 49.3% of its entire portfolio concentrated in Chad. China’s West Africa concentration was also substantial.

These actor-level shifts also reflected a broader change in financing patterns. Gross finance from sovereign donors plus EU Institutions contracted by 10.0%, while that from other multilateral institutions increased by 19.7%. As a result, total reported gross official finance rose by 2.6%, even as net ODA across all reported providers declined by 3.4%.

Official bilateral lending

China remained by far Africa’s* largest official bilateral creditor, with $62.2 billion in claims at the end of 2024, equivalent to 41.8% of bilateral PPG debt.

However, its position in the second period was one of net repayment.

European creditors operated through two distinct channels. EU Member States held $30.9 billion, while European institutions held a further $13.8 billion, classified as multilateral debt. Gulf creditors held $23.6 billion, concentrated in Egypt and Sudan(5) Russia’s expanding portfolio was even more concentrated, with around 91% of its claims in Egypt, while the US remained a small, largely legacy bilateral creditor.

Creditor portfolios also differed in their terms. Japan and South Korea stood out for highly concessional lending, while around 37% of EU Member States’ bilateral stock was concessional. Recorded concessional shares were considerably lower for China and Gulf creditors.(6)

This comes against a broader shift in Africa’s external debt. Between 2019 and 2024, it rose by 21% to $1,175.5 billion, while its composition shifted sharply towards multilateral creditors. Multilateral PPG claims rose by 49% to $264.5 billion, while official bilateral debt remained broadly flat.

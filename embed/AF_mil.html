Arms transfers1

Between 2016 and 2025, Russia, France, and the US were the largest suppliers of arms to African countries, followed by Germany and China. Taken together, the top five suppliers accounted for about 80% of registered transfers.

Among the major suppliers, China supplied weapons to the widest number of recipients in Africa (34 countries and the African Union). In terms of geographical reach, the UAE supplied 28 countries, France 27, the US 25 and Russia 212.

Egypt is the main recipients of arms transfers accounting alone for 43% of registered arms transfers to the continent between 2016 and 2025.

Morocco received 57% of US arms transfers to the continent and 63% of Israeli arms transfers, while Algeria received about 35% of Chinese arms transfers to the continent and about 47.3% of Russian arms transfers.

Since 2022, Morocco also received the biggest share of Israeli’s transfers to African countries, passing from zero transfers in 2018–2021 to 376 of Israel’s 507 TIV in 2022–25 (74%).

Overall, seven countries increased their arms transfers to African countries between 2018–2021 and 2022–2025: China (+51%), France (+37%), Germany (+23%), Türkiye (+114%), the US (+29%), Israel (+881%) and Iran (+367%). Although Israel and Iran recorded the largest percentage increases, both started from low levels. Israel’s transfers rose from 51.6 to 506.9 TIV, while Iran’s increased from 0.2 to 0.7 TIV. By contrast, arms transfers from Russia and Italy fell sharply, by 83% and 81%, respectively.

However, the increase varied across African regions. China increased its transfers to West Africa (+108%) and North Africa (+88%); France, Germany and the US to North Africa; Iran to East Africa but with limited volumes; Israel to North and East Africa; and Türkiye to West and East Africa.

Focusing on Sub-Saharan Africa alone, the picture changes slightly3.

China is the leading arms supplier to Sub-Saharan countries, followed by Russia, France, and Türkiye. Transfers from China and Russia are almost half of the total transfers to Sub-Saharan Africa in the considered period.

While Chinese transfers are widespread (31 recipients), the Russian ones are more concentrated (19 recipients). Russia’s largest recipients were Angola with 39% of its Sub-Saharan transfers, Ethiopia (14%) and Mali (13.5%).

In Sub-Saharan Africa, Angola and Nigeria received the largest volume of arms transfers, mostly from Russia and China respectively.

Türkiye, China and Iran supplied armed drones to different countries. Türkiye has the most widespread network of clients in Sub-Saharan Africa (Burkina Faso, Chad, Djibouti, Ethiopia, Kenya, Mali, Niger, Sudan and Togo). China transferred armed drones to DRC, Ethiopia and Nigeria. Iran transferred armed drones only to Ethiopia and Sudan respectively in 2021 and 2023.

Military bases4

Beyond arms transfers, some external actors have also established a physical presence through military bases or deployments.

Military bases are strongly concentrated in Djibouti (US, China, France, Italy and Japan). However, the US also uses lighter footprint presence in the region, such as Camp Simba, a cooperative security location near Manda Bay, Kenya, where US and Kenyan forces cooperate for regional security.

Deployments5

However, the presence of military bases provides only a partial picture of foreign military involvement in Africa. Examining foreign military deployments offers a more comprehensive understanding of foreign military presence in the region.

The largest bilateral deployments in Africa are associated with military bases, particularly in Djibouti, where the US has 4,000 personnel deployed, France 1,500 and China 400. Türkiye’s military base in Somalia also hosts 800 personnel.

Some EU Member states maintain smaller deployments where bilateral agreements have established bilateral mission. Thus, France maintains small deployments in Cote d’Ivoire (80 personnel), Gabon (100), and Senegal (350). Italy deploys bilateral missions in Libya (160 personnel) and Niger (250).

Unlike traditional military deployments, Russian private military companies (PMCs)6 – now mostly absorbed by Africa Corps which is closely linked to the Russian Ministry of Defence – operate under a range of mandates, including training and securing investments. They also participate in military operations alongside local forces. They currently deploy mostly in the Sahel countries, Libya and the Central African Republic. Russian PMCs reportedly have large deployments, particularly in Libya (approximately 3,500 personnel), Mali (approximately 1,500) and the Central African Republic (approximately 1,500).

Several external actors considered, also deploy military personnel to the UN peacekeeping missions on the continent.

In 2025, the US and Russia had very limited deployments to UN missions, respectively 18 and 24 personnel overall, while EU Member States deployed 267 personnel, with Portugal alone deploying 215 personnel to MINUSCA in the Central African Republic.

In contrast, India was the largest contributor to UN missions in Africa among the considered actors. In 2025, it deployed 4081 personnel overall with the biggest deployments to MONUSCO in the DRC (1,076 personnel) and UNMISS in South Sudan (2,401), where China also deploys a large contingent (1,053).

Chinese deployments in 2025 were the second largest among the considered countries with overall 1360 personnel of which 1053 to UNMISS in South Sudan and 285 to UNISFA, Abyei*8.

Military exercises7

Military exercises add another layer to understanding the extent and depth of military ties. They show how closely external actors work with regional forces. The Gulf of Guinea and the Gulf of Aden/Western Indian Ocean stand out as particularly crowded maritime areas, where exercises concentrate.

Between January 2024 and July 2026, we registered 55 joint military exercises between external partners and African countries. About 52% of these exercises included navies or a maritime security component. 26% were exercises interforce and 7% air forces and 11% land or special forces.

15 exercises were US-led, including multilateral exercises such as African Lion and Obangame express to which EU member states also participate.

China co-organised four exercises: two air forces exercises with Egypt (Eagles of civilisation), one naval with South Africa and other BRICS countries (Will for Peace), and one with Mozambique and Tanzania (Peace and Unity).

Russia run a joint bilateral exercise with Egypt in 2025 involving naval and air forces (Bridge of friendship).

In 2025, India also launched the Africa-India Key Maritime Engagement (Aikeyme), a naval exercise in the Indian Ocean to which several African coastal states participated (Tanzania, Comoros, Djibouti, Kenya, Madagascar, Mauritius, Mozambique, Seychelles, South Africa). In addition, India held bilateral exercises with Egypt and Seychelles and a trilateral one with South Africa and Brazil (IBSAMAR VIII).

The EU and its Member States organised about 29 joint exercises in Africa or in its continental waters.

EUNAVFOR ATALANTA continued its joint exercises with African coastal states, such as Djibouti, Kenya and Seychelles, and expanded its international cooperation through joint exercises with India and Japan. EUNAVFOR ASPIDES also launched joint exercises with Djibouti and some Passex exercises.

Among EU Member States, France co-organised the largest number of exercises with Morocco, Senegal, Gabon and countries in the Gulf of Guinea and the Yaoundé architecture. Also, Spain co-organised seven naval exercises mainly within the framework of the Coordinated Maritime Presence in the Gulf of Guinea.

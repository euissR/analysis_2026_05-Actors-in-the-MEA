Levers of influence in Africa and the Middle East and the Gulf maps how 16 external actors engage through diplomacy, soft power, economic ties and military cooperation in two regions of strategic importance to the EU: Africa, and the Middle East and the Gulf. The picture is neither one of Western retreat nor a wholesale shift from West to East. Although trade is gradually tilting towards Asia - most clearly in the Middle East and the Gulf – no single rising challenger is displacing established partners across the board. Instead, external influence is crowded and layered. Actors specialise by dimension, subregion and, often, a small set of partner countries. No single actor leads across all four dimensions, while formal partnerships, diplomatic activity, trade, finance, military presence and voting alignment often point in different directions. Headline rivalries therefore capture only part of how external influence is distributed.

The EU remains an incumbent heavyweight, but it is largely holding its ground rather than gaining it. It is Africa’s largest trading partner and largest recorded FDI counterpart, as well as the most active source of high-level visits. Among the external actors tracked, the EU is also Africa’s largest provider of official finance, leading among around two-thirds of country-coded recipients, and ranks ahead of the United States in the Middle East. There, the EU also runs the largest goods surplus among the actors analysed, while its Member States are the largest contributors to UN peacekeeping missions. Yet this broad presence is not translating into greater relative weight. Trade shares are broadly flat, and European net ODA to Africa fell by almost a fifth. In infrastructure, European sponsor participation remains substantial, but Chinese-registered suppliers capture a much larger share of the value of foreign World Bank IPF awards associated with infrastructure projects in Africa. At the UN, African states align more often with the EU than with Russia on Ukraine, but most closely with China, India and Türkiye on decolonisation. On international tax governance, African positions are closer to those of China, India and Russia. Europe therefore retains unusually broad and deep-rooted relationships across both regions, while facing growing pressure in strategically important sectors from actors able to concentrate resources in particular markets and countries.

Other major actors are strongest in specific domains. The United States remains the dominant military presence and arms supplier in the Middle East and the Gulf and ranks second among the aid providers tracked in both regions. Its commercial footprint, however, is comparatively modest, and its role as an official bilateral creditor is largely a legacy of earlier lending. China combines broad diplomatic coverage in Africa with strong commercial and infrastructure positions. It is the largest goods-trade partner in the Middle East and the Gulf and the second-largest in Africa, while Chinese-registered suppliers receive most of the foreign World Bank infrastructure-award value analysed in Africa. Yet its financial role is also changing. China remains Africa’s largest official bilateral creditor, but average annual net flows turned negative in 2022–2024 as repayments exceeded new disbursements. Its development-finance model also differs markedly from conventional aid: most recorded commitments are OOF-like rather than ODA-like and are heavily concentrated in infrastructure.

Beyond the United States and China, several actors have carved out distinct niches. Japan stands out among the larger donors for substantially expanding aid in both regions, largely through loans, and became Iraq’s largest selected aid provider in 2022–2024. Gulf engagement is prominent but highly concentrated: the UAE accounts for around 85% of the Gulf’s recorded FDI position in Africa and is the main driver of the GCC’s position as the third-largest trading partner among the actors tracked, while individual Gulf donor profiles vary sharply across regions. Türkiye has the widest diplomatic network in the Middle East, India is the largest external contributor to UN peacekeeping missions in Africa, and Russia’s security footprint is increasingly concentrated in private military deployments and a narrower set of arms relationships.

The following analysis traces these patterns across each dimension, asking where the EU’s weight translates into influence and where other actors are redefining the terms of partnership.

Key insights

Chapters

Africa

1

Talking Power

Diplomatic ties and political influence

How foreign powers are reshaping diplomatic ties across the continent. A new generation of embassies, summits, and bilateral frameworks is connecting African states to external patrons whose interests do not always align with those of the continent itself.

2

Shaping Perceptions

Media, education, and cultural reach

Cultural influence, media, and education as tools of foreign engagement. From disinformation networks and state broadcasters to Confucius Institutes, aid organisations, and Gulf-funded religious institutions, soft-power competition is intensifying across Africa.

3

Shaping Markets

Trade and commercial ties

Who trades most with Africa, and where dependence is concentrated. The EU remains the largest partner but is strongest in North Africa, while China leads in sub-Saharan Africa and is gaining ground in green-energy and high-tech goods.

4

Financing Influence

Investment, aid and debt

How external finance creates exposure. Europe remains the largest source of official finance, but its lead is narrowing; China stays Africa’s largest bilateral creditor despite net repayments, and a single actor holds over half of recorded FDI in roughly one in three economies.

5

Anchoring Ties

Infrastructure and construction

Who sponsors and who builds. European sponsors have the largest footprint in privately financed infrastructure, but Chinese-registered firms lead World Bank-financed foreign works awards in 27 of 42 African countries.

6

Securing Interests

Security partnerships and military engagement

Military presence, arms transfers, and security partnerships. Paramilitary deployments, French withdrawal, US base negotiations, and a surge in arms transfers from non-Western suppliers are redrawing the security map of the continent.

Middle East and the Gulf

1

Talking Power

Diplomatic ties and political influence

How foreign powers are reshaping diplomatic ties across the region. From summit diplomacy and bilateral agreements to UN positioning and regional forum engagement, external actors are rewriting the rules of diplomatic engagement across the Middle East.

2

Shaping Perceptions

Media, education, and cultural reach

Cultural influence, media, and education as tools of foreign engagement. State-backed broadcasters, cultural centres, and scholarship programmes are being deployed by competing powers to build long-term affinity and shape narratives across the region.

3

Shaping Markets

Trade and commercial ties

Who trades most with the Middle East and the Gulf, and where dependence is concentrated. China is the largest goods-trade partner, while the EU runs the largest goods surplus among the actors analysed.

4

Financing Influence

Investment, aid and debt

The EU remains a major financial anchor, but its relative position is weakening. Japan is the largest bilateral creditor, and the Gulf states have distinct profiles: Kuwait in FDI, Saudi Arabia in grant-based aid, the UAE in both.

5

Securing Interests

Security partnerships and military engagement

Military presence, arms transfers, and security partnerships. From permanent bases and naval deployments to arms deals and proxy force support, foreign powers are projecting hard power across the region with growing reach and ambition.

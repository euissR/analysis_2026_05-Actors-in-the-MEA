Foreign direct investment

The EU remains the Middle East’s largest recorded FDI counterpart in both income groups. However, its relative position has weakened. Its share fell in both groups, by 5.6 percentage points in the high-income group and by 4.8 percentage points in the lower- and middle-income group. Its recorded stock fell by USD 30 bn in the high-income group2, a fall concentrated in 2024, and was broadly unchanged at USD 13 bn in the lower- and middle-income group.

The United States was the second-largest investor in the high-income group in 2019-21. Its recorded stock rose from USD 81 bn to USD 89 bn,

entirely on account of Israel, where US-linked investment rose by 29% to USD 53 bn, a steady fifth of Israel’s inward stock.

Gulf investors held USD 97 bn (13.4%) of recorded high-income stock in 2022-24, up from USD 79 bn (11.3%) and more than China, India, Japan and South Korea combined.

In the lower- and middle-income group, Gulf investors ranked second, although their share declined from 15.5% to 13.0%.

Their positions were highly concentrated: the UAE and Kuwait accounted for two thirds of recorded Gulf high-income stock, while Kuwait alone represented approximately 85% of the lower- and middle-income position.

Other Asian investors remained relatively minor in the high-income group: China, India, Japan and South Korea together held under 6% of recorded stock in both periods (5.7% and 5.8%).

In the lower- and middle-income economies, however, China strengthened its position from 6.1% to 8.0%, overtaking the United States, whose share fell from 7.3% to 5.1%, while India’s share rose from 0.7% to 1.9%.

Official development finance

Net ODA to the Middle East3 declined by 14.8% between 2019–21 and 2022–24 across all reported official providers. Türkiye accounted for 86.3% of the decline; excluding its contributions, the fall was just 3.2%.

Despite this decline, among the selected reporting donors, Türkiye remained the largest net ODA provider, followed by Europe (EU27 member states and EU Institutions) and the United States, who also reduced their contributions.

Other providers expanded: Japan nearly doubled its net ODA and the UAE more than doubled it, bringing both close to Saudi Arabia, with all three providing approximately USD 3.2–3.5 billion in 2022–24.

Country-level patterns differed***: Japan displaced Europe as Iraq's largest selected provider of net ODA, while Europe retained its lead in Palestine*, where the UAE became the second-largest provider.

Türkiye’s reported assistance was overwhelmingly attributed to Syria and classified as humanitarian.

Grants remained dominant instrument, although loans' share of gross official finance, including other official flows (OOF), rose from 5.9% to 9.7% among state donors and EU Institutions.

Financing models differed considerably: loans accounted for 82.4% of Japan's gross official finance, compared with 19.7% for the UAE and less than 1% for Saudi Arabia.

China followed a different model. Its recorded official-finance commitments were concentrated in Iraq (94.7%), predominantly in the form of debt. Repayment was largely secured through arrangements earmarking oil-export revenues, with 93.1% of the commitments classified as collateralised.4

Official bilateral lending

Across the five lower- and middle-income economies covered, bilateral public and publicly guaranteed (PPG) debt fell from USD 22.7 billion in 2019 to USD 17.9 billion in 2024, while multilateral debt rose from USD 14.1 billion to USD 17.4 billion, narrowing the gap between the two.

Japan remained the largest bilateral creditor, with USD 6.21 billion in outstanding claims in 2024, around three quarters of them in Iraq and 72% on concessional terms.

EU member states followed with USD 4.68 billion, down 21.7% since 2019 (largely on account of Iraq). Separately, the European Investment Bank and the European Union held USD 1.49 billion in combined recorded multilateral claims in 2024. The EIB’s own stock declined slightly compared with 2019, while the separately reported European Union claims were concentrated in Jordan.

Other creditors had smaller, more geographically concentrated positions. US claims fell sharply from USD 3.16 billion to USD 0.35 billion, while China's nearly halved to USD 0.69 billion, in both cases largely reflecting declining exposure to Iraq. Russia retained USD 2.05 billion in recorded claims, mostly in Syria and Yemen, although largely frozen debt series complicate their interpretation.

Gulf creditors followed distinct geographical patterns: Saudi Arabia's USD 1.66 billion was concentrated in Yemen, Kuwait's USD 1.11 billion was more widely distributed, while the UAE's smaller but growing USD 0.39 billion position largely reflected increased lending to Jordan.

Creditor portfolios also differed in their lending terms. Concessional debt accounted for 45% of the five economies’ bilateral PPG debt at end-2024, up from 31% in 2019, even as the overall bilateral debt stock declined. Japan accounted for 56% of this concessional debt, with 72% of its own claims qualifying as concessional.

South Korea had an even higher recorded share (82%), but a much smaller portfolio. Reported concessional shares were lower for EU Member States (25%), Gulf creditors (22%) and China (17%), although incomplete reporting, particularly for China, limits direct comparisons.

Outstanding claims, however, tell only part of the story: net lending flows reveal which creditors are still providing financing and which are being repaid.

Japan stands out as the clearest source of continued bilateral lending, with average annual net flows of USD 470 million in 2022–24 (although these turned slightly negative in 2024).

By contrast, EU member states and China recorded net repayments in both periods, while the sharp reduction in US exposure reflected repayments, predominantly by Iraq.

The UAE's expansion was concentrated in a one-off increase in lending to Jordan in 2022 rather than sustained growth across the region.

Overall, bilateral net financial flows to the five economies remained negative, averaging USD 433 million in net repayments annually in 2022–24.

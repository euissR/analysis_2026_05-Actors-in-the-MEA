Financing influence: investment, aid and debt

  • The EU remains a major financial anchor, but its relative position is weakening. It leads in recorded FDI in both income groups and remains a major ODA provider, but its FDI shares and bilateral debt exposure have declined.
  • Japan stands out as the largest bilateral creditor and clearest source of continued lending.
  • Gulf states have distinct financial profiles. Kuwait dominates Gulf FDI in lower- and middle-income economies (85% of Gulf total). Saudi Arabia is a major, predominantly grant-based ODA provider, while the UAE combines substantial investment positions with rapidly expanding aid.

As with trade, external financial exposure does not translate directly into influence. What matters is not only how much money flows, but also through which channels, how concentrated those flows are and what kind of relationships they create. Foreign direct investment (FDI) creates long-term commercial interests by embedding foreign firms in local economies. Official development assistance (ODA) links donor countries to governments through financial support for budgets, services and infrastructure. Remittances create leverage by giving host countries the power to restrict migrants’ access to their labour markets.

For high-income economies, FDI dominates external finance across the selected comparison measures: they provide rather than receive ODA and are predominantly sources of, rather than destinations for, remittances. Between 2019-2021 and 2022-2024, their average annual FDI inflows rose from USD 52.8 bn to USD 90.3 bn.

In lower- and middle-income countries, remittances1 and ODA carry far greater weight, while net FDI is weak (Iraq alone turns an otherwise positive four-country balance into an average annual net outflow of USD 2.3 billion). ODA remains especially important for conflict-affected economies, averaging USD 13.3 bn a year across the six recipients in 2022–2024.

Debt adds a different form of exposure, reflecting accumulated obligations rather than new financing. Collectively, over 2022-2024 the five middle-income borrowers2 covered paid external creditors USD 18.5 bn more in principal and interest than they received in new borrowing.

Foreign direct investment

The EU remains the Middle East’s largest recorded FDI counterpart in both income groups. However, its relative position has weakened. While recorded FDI stock rose slightly in high-income group, it declined in the low- and middle-income economies. In both groups, EU’s share of inward FDI stock fell: from 42.6% to 37.1% in the former and from 41.4% to 38.6% in the latter.

The United States ranked second in the high-income group, rising from USD 80 bn to USD 89 bn and from 11.4% to 12.3% of stock between the two periods.

Almost all of the increase came from Israel, where US-linked investment rose by 29% to USD 53 bn, while retaining a steady share of about one fifth.

Gulf investors ranked third in the high-income group, with USD 81bn (11.1%) of recorded stock, more than China, India and Japan combined.

They ranked second in the lower- and middle-income group, although their share declined from 15.4% to 12.9%.

Their positions were highly concentrated: the UAE and Kuwait accounted for two thirds of the high-income stock, while Kuwait alone represented approximately 85% of the lower- and middle-income position.

Other Asian investors remained relatively minor in the high-income group: China, India, Japan and South Korea together increased their share only marginally, from 5.2% to 5.4%.

In the lower- and middle-income economies, however, China strengthened its position from 6.1% to 8.0%, overtaking the US, while India's share rose from 0.7% to 1.9%.

Official development finance

Net ODA to the Middle East3 declined by 14.6% between 2019–21 and 2022–24 across all reported official providers. Türkiye accounted for 81.9% of the decline; excluding its contributions, the fall was just 4.1%.

Despite that, among the selected reporting donors, Türkiye remained the largest net ODA provider, followed by Europe (EU27 member states and EU Institutions) and the United States, who also reduced their contributions.

Other providers expanded: Japan nearly doubled its net ODA and the UAE more than doubled it, bringing both close to Saudi Arabia, with all three providing approximately USD 3.2–3.5 billion in 2022–24.

Country-level patterns differed***: Japan displaced Europe as Iraq's largest selected provider of net ODA, while Europe retained its lead in Palestine*, where the UAE became the second-largest provider.

Türkiye’s reported assistance was overwhelmingly attributed to Syria and classified as humanitarian.

Grants remained dominant instrument, although loans' share of gross official finance, including other official flows (OOF), rose from 5.9% to 9.7% among state donors and EU Institutions.

Financing models differed considerably: loans accounted for 82.4% of Japan's gross official finance, compared with 19.7% for the UAE and less than 1% for Saudi Arabia.

China followed a different model. Its recorded official-finance commitments were concentrated in Iraq (94.7%), predominantly in the form of debt. Repayment was largely secured through arrangements earmarking oil-export revenues, with 93.1% of the commitments classified as collateralised.4

Official bilateral lending

Across the five lower- and middle-income economies covered, bilateral public and publicly guaranteed (PPG) debt fell from USD 22.7 billion in 2019 to USD 17.9 billion in 2024, while multilateral debt rose from USD 14.1 billion to USD 17.4 billion, narrowing the gap between the two.

Japan remained the largest bilateral creditor, with USD 6.21 billion in outstanding claims in 2024, around three quarters of them in Iraq and 72% on concessional terms.

EU member states followed with USD 4.68 billion, down 21.7% since 2019 (largely on account of Iraq). Meanwhile, claims held by the European Investment Bank and EU institutions, classified as multilateral, rose by 83% to USD 1.49 billion, driven by Jordan.

Other creditors had smaller, more geographically concentrated positions. US claims fell sharply from USD 3.16 billion to USD 0.35 billion, while China's nearly halved to USD 0.69 billion, in both cases largely reflecting declining exposure to Iraq. Russia retained USD 2.05 billion in recorded claims, mostly in Syria and Yemen, although largely frozen debt series complicate their interpretation.

Gulf creditors followed distinct geographical patterns: Saudi Arabia's USD 1.66 billion was concentrated in Yemen, Kuwait's USD 1.11 billion was more widely distributed, while the UAE's smaller but growing USD 0.39 billion position largely reflected increased lending to Jordan.

Outstanding claims, however, tell only part of the story: net lending flows reveal which creditors are still providing financing and which are being repaid.

Japan stands out as the clearest source of continued bilateral lending, with average annual net flows of USD 470 million in 2022–24 (although these turned slightly negative in 2024).

By contrast, EU member states and China recorded net repayments in both periods, while the sharp reduction in US exposure reflected repayments, predominantly by Iraq.

The UAE's expansion was concentrated in a one-off increase in lending to Jordan in 2022 rather than sustained growth across the region.

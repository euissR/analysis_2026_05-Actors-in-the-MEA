Financing influence: investment, aid and debt

  • The EU remains a major financial anchor, but its relative position is weakening. It leads in recorded FDI in both income groups and remains a major ODA provider, but its FDI shares and bilateral debt exposure have declined.
  • Japan stands out as the largest bilateral creditor and clearest source of continued lending.
  • Gulf states have distinct financial profiles. Kuwait dominates Gulf FDI in lower- and middle-income economies (85% of Gulf total). Saudi Arabia is a major, predominantly grant-based ODA provider, while the UAE combines substantial investment positions with rapidly expanding aid.

As with trade, external financial exposure does not translate directly into influence. What matters is not only how much money flows, but also through which channels, how concentrated those flows are and what kind of relationships they create. Foreign direct investment (FDI) creates long-term commercial interests by embedding foreign firms in local economies. Official development assistance (ODA) links donor countries to governments through financial support for budgets, services and infrastructure. Remittances create leverage by giving host countries the power to restrict migrants’ access to their labour markets.

For high-income economies, FDI dominates external finance across the selected comparison measures: they provide rather than receive ODA and are predominantly sources of, rather than destinations for, remittances. Between 2019-2021 and 2022-2024, their average annual FDI flows rose from USD 52.8 bn to USD 89.2 bn(1).

In lower- and middle-income countries, remittances(2) and ODA carry far greater weight, while net FDI remains weak. Across the five economies with available data, net inward FDI averaged a negative USD 2.35 billion annually in 2022–24, driven overwhelmingly by Iraq.(3) ODA remains especially important for conflict-affected economies, averaging USD 23.5 bn annually between 2022–2024 across the Middle East(4), although the total is heavily influenced by Türkiye’s reported assistance attributed to Syria.

Debt adds a different form of exposure, reflecting accumulated obligations rather than new financing. Across the five low- and middle-income borrowers covered, net transfers on external debt were negative by an average of USD 6.2 bn annually in 2022–24, meaning that principal and interest exceeded new borrowing. This includes both public and private external debt.

Foreign direct investment

The EU remains the Middle East’s largest recorded FDI counterpart in both income groups. However, its relative position has weakened. Its share fell in both groups, by 5.6 percentage points in the high-income group and by 4.8 percentage points in the lower- and middle-income group. Its recorded stock fell by USD 30 bn in the high-income group(5), a fall concentrated in 2024, and was broadly unchanged at USD 13 bn in the lower- and middle-income group.

The United States was the second-largest investor in the high-income group in 2019-21. Its recorded stock rose from USD 81 bn to USD 89 bn,

entirely on account of Israel, where US-linked investment rose by 29% to USD 53 bn, a steady fifth of Israel’s inward stock.

Gulf investors held USD 97 bn (13.4%) of recorded high-income stock in 2022-24, up from USD 79 bn (11.3%) and more than China, India, Japan and South Korea combined.

In the lower- and middle-income group, Gulf investors ranked second, although their share declined from 15.5% to 13.0%.

Their positions were highly concentrated: the UAE and Kuwait accounted for two thirds of recorded Gulf high-income stock, while Kuwait alone represented approximately 85% of the lower- and middle-income position.

Other Asian investors remained relatively minor in the high-income group: China, India, Japan and South Korea together held under 6% of recorded stock in both periods (5.7% and 5.8%).

In the lower- and middle-income economies, however, China strengthened its position from 6.1% to 8.0%, overtaking the United States, whose share fell from 7.3% to 5.1%, while India’s share rose from 0.7% to 1.9%.

Official development finance

Net ODA to the Middle East(6) declined by 14.8% between 2019–21 and 2022–24 across all reported official providers. Türkiye accounted for 86.3% of the decline; excluding its contributions, the fall was just 3.2%.

Despite this decline, among the selected reporting donors, Türkiye remained the largest net ODA provider, followed by Europe (EU27 member states and EU Institutions) and the United States, who also reduced their contributions.

Other providers expanded: Japan nearly doubled its net ODA and the UAE more than doubled it, bringing both close to Saudi Arabia, with all three providing approximately USD 3.2–3.5 billion in 2022–24.

Country-level patterns differed among selected providers with recipient-level data. Japan displaced Europe as Iraq's largest selected provider of net ODA, while Europe retained its lead in Palestine*, where the UAE became the second-largest provider.

Türkiye’s reported assistance was overwhelmingly attributed to Syria and classified as humanitarian.

Grants remained dominant instrument, although loans' share of gross official finance(7) rose from 5.9% to 9.7% among state donors and EU Institutions.

Financing models differed considerably, with Japan relying much more heavily on loans (82.4%) than the UAE (19.7%) or Saudi Arabia (0.7%).

China followed a different model. Its recorded official-finance commitments were concentrated in Iraq (94.7%), predominantly in the form of non-concessional loans. Repayment was largely secured through arrangements earmarking oil-export revenues.(8)

Official bilateral lending

Across the five lower- and middle-income economies covered, bilateral public and publicly guaranteed (PPG) debt fell from USD 22.7 billion in 2019 to USD 17.9 billion in 2024, while multilateral debt rose from USD 14.1 billion to USD 17.4 billion, narrowing the gap between the two.

Japan remained the largest bilateral creditor, with USD 6.21 billion in outstanding claims in 2024, around three quarters of them in Iraq and 72% on concessional terms.

EU member states followed with USD 4.68 billion, down 21.7% since 2019 (largely on account of Iraq). Separately, the European Investment Bank and the European Union held USD 1.49 billion in combined recorded multilateral claims in 2024. The EIB’s own stock declined slightly compared with 2019, while the separately reported European Union claims were concentrated in Jordan.

Other creditors had smaller, more geographically concentrated positions. US claims fell sharply from USD 3.16 billion to USD 0.35 billion, while China's nearly halved to USD 0.69 billion, in both cases largely reflecting declining exposure to Iraq. Russia retained USD 2.05 billion in recorded claims, mostly in Syria and Yemen, although largely frozen debt series complicate their interpretation.

Gulf creditors followed distinct geographical patterns: Saudi Arabia's USD 1.66 billion was concentrated in Yemen, Kuwait's USD 1.11 billion was more widely distributed, while the UAE's smaller but growing USD 0.39 billion position largely reflected increased lending to Jordan.

Creditor portfolios also differed in their lending terms. Concessional debt accounted for 45% of the five economies’ bilateral PPG debt at end-2024, up from 31% in 2019, even as the overall bilateral debt stock declined. Japan accounted for 56% of this concessional debt, with 72% of its own claims qualifying as concessional.

South Korea had an even higher recorded share (82%), but a much smaller portfolio. Reported concessional shares were lower for EU Member States (25%), Gulf creditors (22%) and China (17%), although incomplete reporting, particularly for China, limits direct comparisons.

Outstanding claims, however, tell only part of the story: net lending flows reveal which creditors are still providing financing and which are being repaid.

Japan stands out as the clearest source of continued bilateral lending, with average annual net flows of USD 470 million in 2022–24 (although these turned slightly negative in 2024).

By contrast, EU member states and China recorded net repayments in both periods, while the sharp reduction in US exposure reflected repayments, predominantly by Iraq.

The UAE's expansion was concentrated in a one-off increase in lending to Jordan in 2022 rather than sustained growth across the region.

Overall, bilateral net financial flows to the five economies remained negative, averaging USD 433 million in net repayments annually in 2022–24.

The EU remains a major financial anchor, leading in recorded FDI stocks in both income groups and retaining an important role in development assistance. However, its recorded FDI shares declined in both groups, although reporting discontinuities complicate interpretation of the underlying trend. Net ODA from EU Member States and EU institutions fell by about 14% between 2019-21 and 2022-24, while Member States’ bilateral debt claims declined by roughly a fifth.

Other actors occupy distinct financial positions. Japan is the largest bilateral creditor, with USD 6.2bn in outstanding claims, most on concessional terms. It also recorded positive average net lending in 2022–24, although flows turned slightly negative in 2024. Gulf states combine different forms of engagement: Kuwait dominates recorded Gulf investment in lower- and middle-income economies, Saudi Arabia provides largely grant-based aid, and the UAE has substantially expanded its ODA alongside its investment presence.

The US also reduced its assistance, while its bilateral debt claims fell from USD 3.16bn to USD 0.35bn, largely reflecting repayments by Iraq. China’s official-finance commitments followed yet another model: concentrated in Iraq, predominantly debt-related and largely secured against oil-export revenues.

Financial exposure therefore does not follow a single hierarchy. The EU retains a broad presence, but other actors occupy more specialised positions through investment, grants and lending. These relationships create different forms of dependence and potential leverage, shaped by the scale, terms and geographic concentration of finance, as well as the availability of alternative sources.

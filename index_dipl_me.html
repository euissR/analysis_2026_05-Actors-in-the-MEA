Talking power: diplomatic ties and political influence

  • Türkiye has the most extensive network of diplomatic missions in the region, while Israel has the smallest. The EU is expanding its diplomatic footprint with the 10th Delegation in the region, Oman, due to open following the signature of the establishment agreement in July 2026(1).
  • External actors engage differently across the Middle East and the Gulf. The US and Türkiye made the most high-level visits (110 and 101 respectively) between 2019 and July 2026, followed by Iran and the EU, with each prioritising different countries.
  • Formal partnerships are concentrated in the Gulf states and Israel, with the UAE and Saudi Arabia courting, or being courted by, several actors at once.
  • Dedicated summits show uneven momentum: the EU and the US have established formats with the GCC countries, while China and Russia have postponed summits with Arab states originally scheduled for 2026 and 2025(2).

Diplomatic relations form the bedrock of international partnerships. As a core component of foreign policy, they offer insights into the importance, breadth and depth of cooperation between states and institutions. Their absence or limited scope may indicate a lack of political will to deepen cooperation. In the Middle East and the Gulf, the presence of embassies and patterns of diplomatic visits reveal varying degrees of political proximity between countries.

This section examines diplomatic ties across five dimensions. First, the presence of embassies provides a measure of the diplomatic footprint of the external actors covered in this analysis across Africa. Second, the number and geographical distribution of visits by heads of state or government and foreign ministers from these external actors between January 2019 and July 2026 offer further insights into their diplomatic engagement with African countries. Third, the existence and frequency of summits between external actors and African countries help gauge the importance attached to these relationships. Fourth, the formalisation of partnerships through specific labels sheds light on external actors’ interest in deepening cooperation, as well as African countries’ preferences and responses. Finally, voting patterns at the UN General Assembly on selected issues, including Russia’s war of aggression against Ukraine and the situation in the Palestinian territories*, provide insights into African countries’ positions on two major international issues and reveal areas of alignment and divergence with external actors.

Diplomatic network

Türkiye has the broadest diplomatic footprint across the region.

Israel has by far the narrowest regional network.

The EU has Delegations in 9 countries and an office in the Palestinian territories*, with a new Delegation planned in Oman following the signature of the establishment agreement in July 2026.

Russia, China and India have 11 diplomatic representations and one office in the Palestinian territories* each.

High-level visits

Beyond the presence of diplomatic missions, frequent high-level visits also reflect the nature of relations between countries and the willingness to invest political capital in the relationship. Between 2019 and July 2026, the US (103), Türkiye (91), Iran (89) and the EU (62) accounted for most high-level visits by external actors(3) to the Middle East and the Gulf. The distribution of these visits reveals different patterns of diplomatic outreach.

US visits focused on Israel, Saudi Arabia and Qatar. Türkiye’s visits focused on Qatar and Saudi Arabia. Iran’s visits focused on Qatar and Syria. The EU’s visits focused on Jordan and the UAE.

However, intra-regional diplomatic engagement was also frequent, with numerous visits by heads of state or government and foreign ministers. GCC countries exchanged 252 high-level visits. Qatar, Kuwait and Bahrain were the most frequent visitors, especially to Saudi Arabia, while Bahrain visited the UAE more often, with Saudi Arabia as its second most frequent destination.

Visits among GCC countries intensified from 2023 onwards. In 2025, Iran also stepped up its diplomatic engagement with the region, particularly Oman, Qatar and Saudi Arabia. In this case, intensification of diplomatic contacts clearly corresponded to a period of heightened regional insecurity.

Partnerships(4)

Stronger bilateral relations are also reflected in how external actors describe their partnerships with regional actors. Several external actors have developed formal partnerships with countries in the Middle East and the Gulf. However, the breadth and depth of formal partnerships varies as does their geographical distribution.

China has comprehensive strategic partnerships with Bahrain, Saudi Arabia and the UAE, and an innovative comprehensive partnership with Israel. Its formal partnerships with Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Oman, Palestine*, Syria and Qatar are less developed and are defined as strategic partnerships.

Most countries in the region are major non-NATO allies of the US (Bahrain, Egypt, Israel, Jordan, Kuwait, Morocco, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia). Israel however is also a strategic partner for the US.

Türkiye has formal partnerships with Qatar, Iraq, Jordan and the UAE, with its Supreme Strategic Committee with Qatar representing the highest level of cooperation.

India has a comprehensive strategic partnership with the UAE and strategic partnerships with Israel, Kuwait, Oman and Saudi Arabia.

Russia has a strategic partnership with the UAE and engages in strategic cooperation with Saudi Arabia.

The EU has Strategic and Comprehensive Partnerships with Jordan and a strategic partnership with the GCC. It is also negotiating individual strategic partnerships with GCC countries: negotiations with Qatar and the UAE were launched in December 2025, followed by Bahrain in July 2026.

Summits

Dedicated summits increasingly signal political eagerness to strengthen partnerships.

The EU and the US have held summits with the GCC countries since 2024 and 2015, respectively. A second EU–GCC summit scheduled to take place in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, in October 2026 has been postponed due to the recent Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia(5).

China launched its summit with the Arab States in 2022; a second summit initially scheduled for 2026 has been postponed sine die. Russia sought to organise a summit with the Arab states in 2025, but it was postponed indefinitely.

Voting at the UN General Assembly

The intensity and depth of diplomatic relations are also often reflected in voting patterns at the UN General Assembly. Abstentionism on UNGA resolutions concerning Ukraine is quite widespread in the region. However, among Gulf countries, the abstention rate decreased from 90% (2016–2021) to 68% (2022–2026), while in the Middle East it increased from 45% to 49%.

Israel has the lowest abstention rate and votes most consistently against Russian positions. Until 2024, Syria consistently voted with Russia; following the fall of the Assad regime, it either abstained or was absent. In the 2016–2022 period, Iran aligned with Russia in every vote, but its alignment rate dropped to 53% after 2022, while its abstention rate reached 40%. China and India aligned with Russia in 90% and 60% of votes, respectively, prior to 2022.

Their abstention rate also increased significantly after 2022, reaching 60% for China and 100% for India.

The US voted with Russia only once, in February 2025, while EU Member States, Japan and Korea consistently voted against Russia’s position.

By contrast, abstentionism on UNGA resolutions on the territories occupied by Israel and on Palestine* is rare in the region.

Over the past decade, countries in the Middle East and the Gulf have consistently voted against Israel’s position on UNGA resolutions on occupied territories, including the Golan Heights and East Jerusalem. The US, however, aligned with Israel in 93% of votes, with its average alignment increasing from 86% to 100% after 2023.

Overall, states in the Gulf and the Middle East – with the exception of Israel – align more often with the EU Member States. After 2023, alignment increased from about 46% to 56%.

EU Member States abstained on average 50% of times prior to 2023 and 36% after, while alignment with Israel rose to 3% after 2023.

On Gaza-related UNGA resolutions since 2023, the US aligned with Israel in 92% of votes, while EU Member States have shown limited alignment with Israel and have frequently abstained.

Countries in the Gulf and the Middle East have aligned with the EU average vote in about 83% of cases. By contrast, Israel and the US have aligned with the EU in only 5% of votes.

The EU is expanding its diplomatic outreach in the region, with a new Delegation due to open in Oman, bringing EU diplomatic representation to 10 of the 12 countries in the region, alongside the EU Office of the Representative to the West Bank and Gaza Strip. Negotiations on strategic partnerships with the GCC countries are also underway.

However, Türkiye already has a diplomatic presence in every country in the region, and ranks second only to the US in terms of the number of high-level visits. These visits reveal distinct patterns of engagement: the EU focuses on Jordan and the UAE, the US on Israel and Saudi Arabia, Türkiye on Qatar and Saudi Arabia, and Iran on Qatar and Iraq.

At the UN General Assembly (UNGA), countries in the region largely abstain on resolutions concerning Ukraine, although the abstention rate among Gulf countries has declined since 2022. Alignment with the EU’s average voting position among countries in the region is around 80 % on Gaza and 60 % on the occupied Palestinian territories*, issues on which the EU’s position diverges from those of the US and Israel.

The EU’s diplomatic influence in the region therefore rests on a few strong relationships and tends to be greatest on issues that resonate with regional interests and perceptions.

Talking power: diplomatic ties and political influence

  • Türkiye has the widest network of diplomatic missions in the region, while Israel has the narrowest. The EU is expanding its diplomatic footprint with the 10th Delegation in the region, Oman, due to open soon.
  • External actors engage differently across the Middle East and the Gulf. The US and Türkiye made the most high-level visits (110 and 101 respectively), followed by Iran and the EU, with each focusing on different countries.
  • Formal partnerships are concentrated in the Gulf states and Israel, with the UAE and Saudi Arabia courting, or being courted by, several actors at once.
  • Dedicated summits show uneven momentum: the EU and the US have established formats with the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, while planned Chinese and Russian summits with Arab states in 2026 and 2025 respectively have been postponed(1).

Diplomatic relations form the foundation of international partnerships. As a core component of foreign policy, they offer insights into the importance, breadth and depth of cooperation between states and institutions. Their absence or limited scope may indicate a lack of political will to deepen cooperation. In the Middle East and the Gulf, the presence of embassies and patterns of diplomatic visits reveal varying degrees of political proximity between countries.

This section examines diplomatic ties through five dimensions. First, the presence of embassies provides a measure of the diplomatic footprint of the external actors covered in this analysis across Africa. Second, the number and geographical distribution of visits by heads of state or government and foreign ministers from these external actors between January 2019 and July 2026 offer further insights into their diplomatic engagement with African countries. Third, the existence and frequency of summits between external actors and African countries help gauge the importance attached to these relationships. Fourth, the formalisation of partnerships through specific labels sheds light on external actors’ interest in deepening cooperation, as well as African countries’ preferences and responses. Finally, voting patterns at the UN General Assembly on selected issues, including Russia’s war of aggression against Ukraine and the Palestinian territories*, provide insights into African countries’ positions on two major international issues and reveal areas of alignment and divergence with external actors.

Diplomatic network

Türkiye has the broadest diplomatic footprint across the region.

Israel has by far the narrowest regional network.

The EU has Delegations in 9 countries and an office in the Palestinian territories*, with a new Delegation planned in Oman.

Russia, China and India have 11 diplomatic representations and one office in the Palestinian territories* each.

High-level visits

Beyond the presence of diplomatic missions, frequent high-level visits also reflect the nature of relations between countries and the willingness to invest political capital in the relation. Between 2019 and July 2026, the US (110), Türkiye (94), Iran (81) and the EU (61) accounted for most high-level visits of external actors(2) to the Middle East and the Gulf. The concentration of visits reveals different patterns of diplomatic outreach.

US visits focused on Israel and Saudi Arabia.

Türkiye’s visits focused on Qatar and Saudi Arabia.

Iran’s visits focused on Qatar and Iraq.

The EU’s visits focused on Jordan and the UAE.

However, intra-regional visits also registered frequent travelling of heads of state or government or foreign ministers.

GCC countries visited each other 265 times. Qatar and Kuwait were the most frequent visitors, especially to Saudi Arabia, while Bahrain visited the UAE more often and Saudi Arabia was the second more frequent destination.

Visits among GCC countries intensified in 2024–2025. Around the same period, in 2025, Iran also increased its visits to the region, particularly Oman, Qatar and Saudi Arabia. In this case, intensification of diplomatic contacts clearly corresponded to a period of increased insecurity in the region.

Partnerships(3)

Stronger bilateral relations are also reflected in how external actors describe their partnerships with regional actors. Several external actors have developed formal partnerships with countries in the Middle East and the Gulf. However, the breadth and depth of formal partnerships varies as does their geographical distribution.

China has comprehensive strategic partnerships with Bahrain, Saudi Arabia and the UAE, and an innovative comprehensive partnership with Israel.

Less developed are the formal partnerships with Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Oman, Palestine*, Syria and Qatar that are defined as strategic.

Most countries in the region are US major non-NATO allies (Bahrain, Egypt, Israel, Jordan, Kuwait, Morocco, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia). Israel though is also a strategic partner for the US.

Türkiye has formal partnerships with Qatar, Iraq, Jordan and the UAE, with its Supreme Strategic Committee with Qatar representing the highest level.

India has a comprehensive strategic partnership with the UAE and strategic partnerships with Israel, Kuwait, Oman and Saudi Arabia.

Russia has a strategic partnership with the UAE and strategic cooperation with Saudi Arabia.

The EU has Strategic and Comprehensive Partnerships with Jordan and a strategic partnership with the GCC. It is also negotiating individual strategic partnerships with GCC countries: negotiations with Qatar and the UAE were launched in December 2025, followed by Bahrain in July 2026.

Summits

Dedicated summits increasingly signal political eagerness to strengthen partnerships.

The EU and the US have held summits with the GCC countries since 2024 and 2015, respectively. A second EU-GCC summit is scheduled in 2026.

China launched its summit with the Arab States in 2022, a second summit initially scheduled for 2026 has been postponed sine die.

Russia sought to organise a summit with the Arab states, but it was postponed indefinitely.

Voting at the UN General Assembly

The intensity and depth of diplomatic relations are also often reflected in voting patterns at the UN General Assembly. Abstentionism on UNGA resolutions pertaining Ukraine is quite widespread in the region.

At the same time, among Gulf countries it decreased from 90% (2016-2021) to 68% (2022-2026), while in the Middle East it increased from 45% to 49%.

Israel is the country that abstains less and that more consistently vote against Russian positions.

Until 2024, Syria consistently voted with Russia; following the fall of the Assad regime, it either abstained or was absent.

In the 2016-2022 period, Iran aligned with Russia at every vote, but alignment rate dropped to 53% after 2022 as its abstention rate reached 40%.

China and India aligned with Russia 90% and 60% of times respectively prior to 2022.

Their abstention rate also increased significantly after 2022, reaching 60% for China and 100% for India.

The US voted with Russia only once, in February 2025, while EU Member States, Japan and Korea consistently voted against Russia’s position.

On the contrary, abstentionism in the region is very limited for UNGA resolutions on Israeli occupied territories and Palestine*.

Countries in the Middle East and the Gulf consistently voted against Israel’s position on UNGA resolutions on Israeli occupied territories, including the Golan Heights and East Jerusalem over the past ten years.

On the contrary, the US aligned with Israel in 93% of votes, increasing average alignment from 86% to 100% after 2023.

Overall, states in the Gulf and the Middle East – with the exceptions of Israel – align more often with the EU member states. After 2023, alignment increased from about 46% to about 56%.

EU member states abstained on average 50% of times prior to 2023 and 36% after, while alignment with Israel increased to 3% after 2023.

On Gaza-related UNGA resolutions since 2023, the US aligned with Israel in 92% of votes, while EU Member States only partially aligned with Israel and frequently abstained.

Countries in the Gulf and the Middle East align with the EU average vote in about 83% of cases.

On the contrary, Israel and the US align with the EU in 5% of votes.

The EU is expanding its diplomatic outreach in the region, with an additional Delegation due to open in Oman, bringing EU diplomatic representation to 10 of the 12 countries in the region, and the EU office of the Representative to the West Bank and Gaza strip, and negotiations under way on strategic partnerships with the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries.

However, Türkiye already has a diplomatic presence in every country in the region, and its number of high-level visits is second only to that of the US. These visits reveal distinct patterns of engagement: the EU focuses on Jordan and the UAE, the US on Israel and Saudi Arabia, Türkiye on Qatar and Saudi Arabia, and Iran on Qatar and Iraq.

At the UN General Assembly (UNGA), countries in the region largely abstain on resolutions concerning Ukraine, although the abstention rate among Gulf countries has declined since 2022. Alignment with the EU’s average voting position among countries in the region is around 80 % on Gaza and 60 % on the occupied Palestinian territories*, issues on which the EU’s position diverges from those of the US and Israel.

The EU’s diplomatic influence in the region therefore rests on a few strong relationships and tends to be greatest on issues that resonate with regional interests and perceptions.

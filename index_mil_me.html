Securing interests: military engagement

Key takeaways

  • The US is the main security partner in the Middle East and the Gulf. It has been the main arms supplier to the region, and it maintained the largest force on the ground (29,000 personnel in 2025) until its withdrawal from Iraq in October 2026. It also has the widest footprint in joint exercises. Other actors play narrower, more specialised roles.
  • In 2025, EU Member States were the largest contributors to UN peacekeeping missions and the NATO Mission to Iraq, while Türkiye had a large bilateral deployment in Iraq. However, in September 2026, deployments in Iraq reduced with the withdrawal of US troops and the agreement between Türkiye and Iraq for handing over bases in Northern Iraq to Baghdad(1).
  • France and India have developed their own military cooperation networks in the region, notably through bilateral exercises particularly with the UAE and Oman.

Introduction

Military cooperation offers a distinct lens through which to understand how partnerships between Middle Eastern and Gulf countries and external actors are evolving. In a region characterised by persistent security tensions, regional rivalries and the strategic importance of energy infrastructure and maritime routes, military cooperation reflects shared security interests and may signal a deeper degree of political trust than other forms of engagement, particularly when it involves the presence of foreign military personnel or infrastructure. Overall, regional actors particularly in the Gulf are clearly leaning towards taking more responsibility for their own security.

This section analyses military cooperation through four layers: arms transfers, military bases, deployments and joint exercises. These layers capture different forms and degrees of military engagement. First, arms transfers show who equips regional forces, a relation that pinpoints to trade links and it is often transactional. Secondly, the presence of military bases that signal a longer-term engagement. In this analysis, we focus on established bases, and we exclude lighter footprints based on security forces embedded in local bases as they may move quickly. Thirdly, deployments show who is present on the ground and complement the picture of physical presence displayed with the military bases. Deployments can be bilateral or embedded in multilateral missions, a significant difference that is reflected in our data. Finally, military exercises show who trains with whom and provide an indication of operational cooperation, interoperability and, potentially, a higher degree of mutual trust. This section excludes ongoing military operations in the region.

Arms transfers(2)

The US has been the main arms provider to the region, particularly to Saudi Arabia, Qatar and the UAE. France and Italy followed with Qatar as main recipient.

Arms transfers from the US were highest in 2016, those from Italy and Germany peaked in 2022, from France in 2021. In the following years, most transfers from these actors declined, but transfers from the US to the region increased again in 2025 but to lower levels than in 2016.

In 2019, Russia supplied arms to Iraq and Syria, but it has no recorded transfers to the Middle East and the Gulf in the period 2022–2025(3).

Israel supplied air defence missiles to the UAE in 2022, and armoured vehicles to Iraq in 2023.

Between 2018–2021 and 2022–2025, the three largest increases of arms transfers to the Gulf were from Italy (+261%, mainly to Qatar and Kuwait), Spain (+623%, mainly to Saudi Arabia), and South Korea (+1,564%, mainly to the UAE).

Between 2018–2021 and 2022–2025, arms transfers to the Middle East fell overall (−19%) mainly due to the end of Russian transfers and a decline in US transfers.

However, some states increased theirs. Transfers from Germany increased from 199 to 877 TIV mainly driven by the delivery of four frigates to Israel in 2022–2023 (729 TIV), France increased its transfers from 9.2 to 142.4 TIV, mainly to Iraq. Türkiye’s transfers increased from 0.8 to 8.2 TIV towards Iraq and Syria and Iranian transfers increased from 30.4 to 35.7 TIV mostly towards the Houthis.

Military bases(4)

However, military cooperation goes beyond arms transfers. The Middle East and the Gulf host several foreign military bases as well as bilateral and multilateral military deployments.

The US has the largest military presence in the Middle East, with several bases across the region.

Deployments(5)

Other countries also had a military presence in the region but mostly through deployments, either bilaterally or to multilateral missions such as UN peacekeeping operations, NATO missions, and the coalition against Daesh.

In 2025, the US had the largest bilateral military deployment in the region, with 29,000 personnel.

EU Member States were the largest contributors to UN peacekeeping missions in the region, with 3,587 personnel deployed in large part to UNIFIL.(6) Italy and Spain provided the largest EU contingents, followed by France. EU Member States also contributed 68 personnel to UNTSO and eight to UNDOF.

UNIFIL included 897 Indian, 484 Chinese, 256 South Korean and 91 Turkish personnel.

EU Member States also provided 567 personnel to the NATO Mission Iraq, compared with 34 from Türkiye and 15 from the US.

However, Türkiye had 4,000 personnel deployed bilaterally to Iraq.

In Iraq, the US contributed 2,000 personnel to the Combined Joint Task Force – Operation Inherent Resolve and another 3,000 in Jordan.

While the EU Member States contributed around 870 personnel to the operation in Iraq, of which 300 from Italy, and 300 French and 150 German personnel in Jordan and 400 Italian personnel in Kuwait.

Military exercises(7)

Military engagement of several countries in the region has also become particularly visible through joint military exercises, mostly bilateral.

Naval exercises occupy a central place in the region’s security architecture together with air defence.

The US has the largest footprint among single external actors.

France and India have established a distinctive network of defence cooperation, France with the UAE and India with the UAE and Oman.

Saudi Arabia and other GCC countries also play prominent organising roles, demonstrating growing regional ownership of Gulf security cooperation.

The US remains the main security partner in the region. Even after the end of Operation Inherent Resolve in Iraq on 30 September 2026, US military deployments remain the largest in the region, based on 2025 figures. The US was also the largest arms supplier between 2016 and 2025. Other actors increased their arms transfers to the Gulf (Italy, Spain and South Korea) and the Middle East (France and Germany), although most of these transfers declined in 2024–2025. The EU’s distinctive role in the region lies in its Member States’ position as the largest contributors to UN peacekeeping missions, particularly the United Nations Interim Force in Lebanon (UNIFIL), whose mandate expires in December 2026. The EU has also announced a Common Security and Defence Policy (CSDP) mission to Lebanon(8), signalling its continued commitment. Military exercises further demonstrate the leading role of the US among external actors. However, Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries are increasingly organising exercises with multiple partners, including EU Member States and India, reflecting growing regional ownership of security cooperation in the Gulf.

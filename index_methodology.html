Methodological note

This study maps the documented presence of selected external actors across Africa, the Middle East and the Gulf and, to the extent possible, assesses the scale of their engagement across the following dimensions: diplomacy; soft power; trade; financial ties; infrastructure investment and military cooperation.

Diplomatic ties are assessed through five indicators: the presence of embassies; the number and geographical distribution of visits by heads of state or government and foreign ministers from these external actors between January 2019 and July 2026; the existence and frequency of summits; the formalisation of partnerships through specific designations; and voting patterns at the UN General Assembly (UNGA) on internationally contested issues that have been the subject of UNGA resolutions, such as Russia’s war of aggression against Ukraine and territories occupied by Israel . Other important issues, such as the conflict in Sudan, taxation and decolonisation, were considered but ultimately excluded either because they were not the subject of a vote at the UNGA (as in the case of Sudan) or would have required more detailed analysis than the scope of this publication allows.

Soft power is understood as the capacity of countries or entities to attract or influence others through the appeal of their culture, ideas and preferences while cultivating and projecting a positive image of themselves. This dimension is assessed through several indicators: the geographical distribution of development cooperation and humanitarian aid activities by country; cultural cooperation, reflected in the presence of language and cultural institutes established by external actors; people-to-people ties, measured by the number of international students from the regions under study enrolled in educational institutions in each of the external actors' countries; the migrant stocks in both directions; and public perceptions, based on surveys conducted by Arab Barometer and Afrobarometer.

The economic presence of selected external actors is explored through merchandise trade, foreign direct investment (FDI), official development assistance (ODA) and other official flows (OOF), external debt, and a range of indicators capturing their involvement in infrastructure development. These indicators reflect different forms of economic engagement: commercial exchanges, accumulated investment positions, official financial commitments or disbursements, and liabilities owed to external creditors.

Infrastructure is covered more selectively due to the lack of comprehensive, comparable cross-country data. Two indicators are used: recorded sponsor participation in infrastructure projects with private participation (World Bank PPI database) and contracts awarded to foreign-registered suppliers under World Bank–financed projects (World Bank’s Contract Awards in Investment Project Financing [Since FY 2020] database). These indicators capture different activities and use different denominators; they are therefore reported separately. They provide a partial picture of external actors’ involvement rather than a measure of their overall share of infrastructure investment, ownership or construction.

Military cooperation has been assessed through arms transfers, military bases and deployments and recent military exercises, while operations linked to ongoing conflicts are excluded.

The selection of indicators was guided by the project's objectives and the availability of sufficiently comprehensive and consistent data to support comparisons across countries, external actors and time periods. This necessarily favours measurable dimensions of engagement while leaving out others for which comparable evidence is limited or unavailable. The analysis is therefore selective rather than exhaustive: it illustrates important patterns within the indicators covered, but does not encompass the full range of relationships or the factors shaping them.

The indicators are complementary rather than additive: they capture different phenomena, use different units and observation periods, and may partly overlap. Combining them into a single index of influence would require explicit conceptual assumptions, justified weighting and safeguards against double-counting.

Instead, the study considers these indicators together to provide a multidimensional picture of actors’ relative positions, relationships and potential sources of leverage. However, the scale of engagement or dependence does not, in itself, establish political influence or an actor's ability to shape the decisions and behaviour of others. The analysis thus identifies potential channels of influence and constraints on its exercise, rather than measuring the influence actually exerted.

The following sections explain the sources, definitions, coverage, methodological choices and limitations of each indicator.

Scope, definitions and comparability

Geographical coverage(1)

Africa comprises 54 countries grouped into five subregions for the purposes of this study: North Africa; West Africa; Central Africa; East Africa and the Horn of Africa; and Southern Africa and the Indian Ocean. Countries are assigned to subregions according to the project's harmonised country crosswalk. Where a source does not cover all 54 countries or economies, the narrower denominator is specified in the relevant section.

The Middle East and the Gulf comprises 13 economies, which for analytical purposes are divided into two categories in the economic sections: high-income economies (Bahrain, Israel, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates [UAE]) and low- and middle-income economies (Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine*, Syria and Yemen). This classification remains constant throughout the period under analysis and is not adjusted annually according to income threshold. When groupings are narrower or differently constructed, it is clearly stated in the text and explained in respective section below.

External actors and aggregates

This publication analyses 16 external actors with significant or expanding relations with Africa, the Middle East and the Gulf: China, the European Union (EU), the GCC countries, Japan, India, Iran, Israel, Russia, South Korea, Türkiye and the United States. Some of these actors are countries that belong to the region under study.

The EU27 is treated as an aggregate of its 27 Member States, without the United Kingdom (including for pre-2020 analysis). Where specified, the aggregate also includes EU institutions. The six GCC countries are also aggregated member by member where relevant. EU institutions are treated separately from Member States in datasets where they are separately identified; the finance sections specify when Europe refers to EU Member States plus EU institutions. Depending on the indicator, Israel and the GCC may be included as part of the geographical region under study, treated as counterpart actors, or excluded from a particular chapter. Their roles and the corresponding denominators are determined by the indicator.

Shares refer to the world or all-recorded total specified for each indicator, not automatically to the sum of selected external actors; rankings limited to a set of selected actors are described as such.

For trade and financial indicators, where used, the two three-year windows reduce sensitivity to isolated annual observations but do not resolve breaks in country reporting. Monetary figures from different databases are not added together. Changes in nominal trade or debt positions may reflect price, exchange rate, valuation or classification effects rather than changes in underlying activity alone.

Remittance inflows appear only as contextual aggregate flows, drawing on the World Bank/KNOMAD series and related GDP benchmarks; they are not assigned to external counterpart countries. Because recent comparable bilateral remittance data is unavailable, remittances are not included in the actor-level analysis.

Embassy presence

Visits

The database includes all visits by heads of state or government and ministers of foreign affairs of external actors to countries in Africa, the Middle East and the Gulf between January 2019 and July 2026 that could be confirmed through official websites or news outlets. When a delegation included more than one of these officials, the visit is counted only once, under the highest-ranking official. The searches were conducted through news scraping and triangulation but may not have been exhaustive. The coverage period was chosen to allow an analysis over time while taking into account travel restrictions during the Covid-19 pandemic. Overall, the database includes 1 283 entries, each supported by a source link: 50% from official sources (government and institutional websites, state news agencies and public broadcasters), 46% from news media and 3% from research institutes and other sources.

Summits

The summits database covers the 19 regular summit formats at the level of heads of state or government that 12 external actors (China, the EU, France, India, Iran, Italy, Japan, Russia, Saudi Arabia, South Korea, Türkiye and the US) held with partners in Africa, the Middle East and the Gulf: the African Union or African states as a group, the Gulf Cooperation Council, the League of Arab States and the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation. For each format, it records the number of summits held, the year of the first and of the most recent summit, and the next scheduled summit, as of October 2026. Formats launched before the period covered by the visits database are included with their full history.

Formal partnerships

The document ranks the formal partnerships and cooperation mechanisms of 14 actors as High, Medium or Low based on their formal designation, strategic significance and scope of cooperation. Each rank depends on the partnership’s official name and where it sits within that actor’s own hierarchy.

High: elevated strategic relationships, privileged security ties, comprehensive economic agreements and top-level coordination bodies. Examples include the US ‘Major Non-NATO Ally’, Russia’s and South Korea’s ‘Comprehensive Strategic Partnership’, Türkiye’s ‘High-Level Strategic Cooperation Council (HLSCC)’ and the UAE’s ‘Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA)’.

Medium: comprehensive or established strategic partnerships, cooperation councils and joint commissions.

Low: basic or sector-specific partnerships, political consultations and strategic dialogues, such as the EU’s raw-materials and hydrogen partnerships.

The ranking was prepared separately for each actor, meaning that the same partnership designation may correspond to different levels across actors. For the UAE, economic partnership agreements are considered as strategic partnerships because it is the only category for which they formalise partnership agreements.

UNGA Resolutions

Analysis is based on the UN General Assembly voting dataset (1946 to 2025) (https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4060887?ln=en). Voting records for Resolution A/RES/ES-11/10 ‘Support for lasting peace in Ukraine’ (2026) were extracted from the single record (https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4103179?ln=en).

The dataset only includes resolutions for which a vote was recorded. The selected topics concern issues that have complicated the functioning of the UN Security Council and register variation in voting. The possibility of including resolutions on Sudan was considered but ultimately ruled out because these resolutions were adopted without a vote. The selected-country analysis covers 101 countries: 54 African countries, six Middle Eastern countries, six Gulf countries, China, India, Iran, Japan, Russia, South Korea, Türkiye, the US, and the current 27 EU Member States.

The alignment of countries’ voting patterns is calculated against Russia’s votes on UNGA resolutions concerning Ukraine and Israel’s votes on UNGA resolutions concerning the occupied territories and Gaza. Alignment is calculated across all voting records, including those in which a country did not vote. Abstentions are also included, but are not counted as agreement with a vote against a resolution.

Alignment with the EU is calculated as the arithmetic mean of a country’s level of agreement with EU Member States across individual resolutions. For each resolution, agreement is defined as the proportion of participating EU Member States that cast the same vote – yes, no or abstention – as the country in question. EU Member States that do not vote are excluded. If the country in question is an EU Member State, its own vote is also excluded. Resolutions on which the country in question does not vote are excluded from the calculation. Each eligible resolution is weighted equally.

  • A/RES/80/195, The right of the Palestinian people to self-determination, 15 December 2025 (https://docs.un.org/en/A/RES/80/195)
  • A/RES/81/1, Participation by the State of Palestine during the 81st session, 17 September 2026 (https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4128222?ln=en).

Geographical distribution of development agencies, grants and projects

The geographical distribution of external actors’ development and humanitarian cooperation was compiled from the official websites of their development agencies, funds and ministries. When clear data for EU Member States was not available, the OECD ODA profiles were used (Croatia, Greece, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Netherlands, and Slovenia).

The institutions considered include national development agencies (for example Japan’s JICA, South Korea’s KOICA, Türkiye’s TIKA, Germany’s GIZ, France’s AFD, Spain’s AECID and Italy’s AICS), development and humanitarian funds (the Qatar Fund for Development, the Abu Dhabi Fund for Development, the Saudi Fund for Development, the King Salman Humanitarian Aid and Relief Centre and Dubai Cares), financial institutions (the US Development Finance Corporation, India Exim Bank and the European Investment Bank), and ministries (India’s Ministry of External Affairs, which reports development grants, and several EU Member States’ foreign ministries). The dataset covers 10 non-EU actors (India, Israel, Japan, Qatar, Russia, Saudi Arabia, South Korea, Türkiye, the UAE and the US), the EU and 26 EU Member States (no data was found for Cyprus), across 92 countries in Africa, the Middle East and the Gulf.

Presence is defined on the basis of information published by each actor: the existence of a field office (or, for Israel, a development sector within its embassies), reported projects or grants, or a list of partner or priority countries (or, for several Member States, a list of ODA recipient countries) published by the agency, ministry, or reported to the OECD Development cooperation profiles (https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/development-co-operation-profiles.html). The number of countries per actor therefore measures geographical reach, not funding levels, volume of activities or impact, and it is not strictly comparable across actors. Types of cooperation (development, humanitarian, grants, loans, cultural) follow each actor’s own description.

The data is the latest available: India (development grants), 2020; Qatar (Qatar Fund for Development), the UAE (Dubai Cares), Hungary and Slovenia, 2023; the Netherlands, Malta, Lithuania, Greece, Latvia and Croatia, 2024; the UAE (Abu Dhabi Fund for Development), Saudi Arabia and the US, 2025; for all other actors 2026.

Cultural cooperation and language institutes

This indicator measures the physical footprint of actors’ cultural diplomacy, i.e. the institutions through which they teach their language and promote their culture abroad. It is based on a manually compiled database containing institution type and host country. The count reflects the number of institutes, centres or offices located in each African and Middle Eastern/Gulf country. Data was collected from the institutes’ own networks and official directories. These include the Confucius Institute network (China), the Yunus Emre Institute’s list of centres (Türkiye), the Russkiy Mir Foundation and Rossotrudnichestvo directories (Russia), the Indian Council for Cultural Relations (ICCR) map of Indian cultural centres, and the Korean Cultural Centre and King Sejong Institute networks (South Korea). For EU Member States, data was taken from the national institutes’ own country listings. The data reflects the situation in 2026, and no time series has been constructed.

The count includes only institutes with a physical presence in the host country, whether they teach the language, run cultural programmes or both. It does not measure size, budget, number of students or activity, so one institute is counted equally regardless of scale.

International students

Data on international students is drawn from the UNESCO Institute for Statistics (UIS) dataset, ‘Other policy relevant indicators’ on inbound mobility (released in February 2026). The data provides the number of international students enrolled in educational institutions in each of the 16 external actors’ countries, broken down by country of origin. The 67 countries of origin are the 54 African countries and 13 countries of the Middle East and the Gulf, grouped according to the regional classification used throughout the publication.

Figures represent stocks of students in a single reference year, not annual flows. UNESCO defines internationally mobile students as individuals who have crossed an international border to study. Their country of origin is defined as the country where they obtained their upper secondary qualifications (prior education), or alternatively, their country of usual residence or, as a last resort, of citizenship. Distance learning and programmes offered by foreign institutions in the student’s country of origin are excluded.

The reference year is 2023 for most actors, with the following exceptions because the data was the latest available: 2022 for Iran, 2024 for Saudi Arabia (data for 2023 was not available), 2012 for the UAE, 2014 for Israel and 2009 for Kuwait. For the EU, the figures are the sum of those for the Member States, excluding Ireland and the Netherlands, for which no 2023 data was available. UNESCO does not report data for China, which is therefore not included.

Because the reference years differ, comparisons of data between the UAE, Kuwait and Israel and any other external actors have been excluded. However, data for these three countries is presented for illustrative purposes. The data counts students by country of origin as defined by UNESCO and reported by each destination country; reporting definitions and coverage (for example degree-seeking versus exchange students) can vary between countries.

Migrant stocks

International migrant stock estimates are drawn from the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs (UN DESA), ‘International Migrant Stock 2024’ (https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/content/international-migrant-stock). UN DESA assigns a data-type code to migrants' country of residence (destination), rather than to their country of origin. The code indicates the evidence used to estimate that destination country’s international migrant stock. Migrants from the same country of origin may therefore be counted differently across destinations.

Foreign-born (B): based on people born outside the destination country. Foreign citizens (C): based on people who do not hold the destination country’s citizenship. Refugee adjustment (R): UN DESA added counts of refugees, persons in refugee-like situations, asylum seekers or Venezuelans displaced abroad, as applicable. Imputed (I): migrant data was unavailable, and estimates were imputed. Codes can be combined; ‘B R’ does not mean that all migrants are refugees.

Comparability: naturalised foreign-born residents may be included under B but excluded under C; some foreign citizens may have been born locally. Refugee adjustments and imputation introduce further methodological differences. These figures are estimates of migrant stocks, not annual migration flows.

EU Member States, South Korea, Russia, Türkiye and the US mostly report data on foreign-born populations and refugees, while China, India, Iran and Japan report data based on citizenship and refugee populations. As destination countries, 35 African countries report data on foreign-born populations and refugees, 10 on foreign citizenship and refugees, three countries report on citizenship only, two on foreign-born populations only, while two report across three categories (foreign-born, citizens, and refugees) and two only report imputed stocks and refugees (Eritrea and Somalia). Among the 13 Middle Eastern and Gulf destinations covered, estimates from UN DESA’s International Migrant Stock 2024 are predominantly citizenship-based (11 destinations), while two are birthplace-based. Refugee-related adjustments are incorporated for seven destinations; none are classified as imputed.

Perceptions

Africa

Perceptions of external actors’ influence in Africa are drawn from Afrobarometer, a pan-African survey series based on nationally representative, face-to-face interviews with adult citizens. The analysis uses Round 10 (2024/2025), which covers 38 African countries and about 51 000 respondents, and, to assess changes over time, Round 9 (2021/2023). Respondents rate the influence of each actor on their country as ‘very negative’, ‘somewhat negative’, ‘neither positive nor negative’, ‘somewhat positive’ or ‘very positive’ (Q67a–h in Round 10), with ‘refused’ and ‘don’t know’ recorded separately. This study selected China, the US, Russia, India, the EU and, for each country, the former colonial power, where included in the survey.

Country shares are calculated using the household-level within-country weight. ‘Positive’ is the sum of ‘somewhat positive’ and ‘very positive’ responses; ‘negative’ is the sum of ‘somewhat negative’ and ‘very negative’ responses. Shares are calculated across all respondents, including those who answered ‘don’t know’ or refused to answer, so positive and negative shares do not add up to 100%. Regional and continental figures are unweighted averages of country shares, so each country counts equally regardless of population.

The analysis of changes between rounds covers only the 35 countries surveyed in both rounds, and focuses on China and the US, the two external actors included in both. Data on the former colonial power is not available for Liberia, and Guinea-Bissau is covered only in Round 10.

Middle East and the Gulf

Opinions on external actors in the Middle East and the Gulf are drawn from the Arab Barometer, a survey series of nationally representative, face-to-face interviews with adult citizens. The analysis uses the 2025–2026 survey data, with interviews conducted between September and November 2025. Five countries are analysed: Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine*, and Syria.

Two questions are used. The first asks whether respondents have a ‘very favourable’, ‘somewhat favourable’, ‘somewhat unfavourable’ or ‘very unfavourable’ opinion of a given country (Q700B). The second asks respondents to what extent they believe different countries are committed to upholding international law (Q739). Both include ‘don’t know’ and ‘refused to answer’ responses. The actors covered in the first question are China, the EU, France, Germany, Iran, Israel, Qatar, Russia, Saudi Arabia, Türkiye, the UAE and the US, while the second question covers China, the EU, Germany, Iran, Russia, the UN and the US. The question on upholding international law was not asked in Syria, so the country is excluded from the weighting. Only the external actors considered in this analysis have been included in the visuals.

Shares are calculated with the survey weight provided in the dataset, and include all respondents asked about each actor, including those who answered ‘don’t know’ or refused to answer. ‘Favourable’ is the sum of ‘very favourable’ and ‘somewhat favourable’ responses, while ‘unfavourable’ is the sum of ‘very unfavourable’ and ‘somewhat unfavourable’ responses.

Merchandise trade

Source, aggregation and calculation

The principal source is the Growth Lab at Harvard University, International Trade Data (HS12) dataset, harmonised to the six-digit level of the Harmonised System 2012 (HS2012), for the period 2019–2024 (Harvard Dataverse DOI: 10.7910/DVN/YAVJDF). The analysis uses the source's harmonised bilateral merchandise import and export observations and its world totals. Trade in services is excluded. All values are in current USD, and the main comparisons use average annual values for 2019–2021 and 2022–2024.

Total trade is calculated as the sum of imports and exports. An actor's merchandise trade balance with a region under study is calculated as its exports to the region minus its imports from that region; a positive balance indicates a trade surplus for the actor. Non-hydrocarbon balances are calculated by subtracting the project-defined hydrocarbon basket from exports and imports on both sides. Partner shares in total trade are measured against the relevant region's recorded world merchandise trade, rather than trade conducted solely with the actors selected for the project. Supplier shares for a product group are calculated against the region's total world imports of that basket; destination shares are calculated against its total world exports of the same basket.

Regional totals and figures for the EU27 and GCC actors are calculated from country-level observations. Subregional comparisons use the same product filters and time conventions as the region-wide calculations. The source's reconciliation of mirror declarations improves data coverage but does not eliminate uncertainty about underlying customs records, intermediary trade, re-exports or changes in national reporting practices.

Selection of product baskets and analytical limits

The analysis proceeds in two stages. It first examines overall merchandise trade – the scale and direction of bilateral exchanges, partner shares and trade balances – before zooming in on selected groups of goods. Given the project's geographical and thematic breadth, a comprehensive product-by-product assessment was not feasible. The baskets were selected to capture key dimensions of economic security across Africa, the Middle East and the Gulf: energy security and the energy transition (hydrocarbons and selected green energy-related products); food security and agricultural inputs (agri-food, cereals and, for Africa, fertilisers); and technological and industrial security (high-technology products). This selection was informed by contemporary geopolitical developments, observed regional trade patterns and the availability of comparable HS2012 data.

The resulting product-level analysis is illustrative rather than exhaustive. The selected baskets neither encompass every commercially or strategically significant good nor capture trade in services, and they are not a representative sample of merchandise trade as a whole. Their findings identify specific trade concentrations, commercial strengths and potential dependencies, but cannot by themselves establish an actor's overall influence or the full extent of bilateral economic ties. They should therefore be interpreted alongside the aggregate trade analysis, which provides a broader context.

Product classification

Basket Operational definition Scope and qualifications
Hydrocarbons 13 project-selected HS2012 six-digit lines under headings 2709, 2710 and 2711. Covers crude petroleum, refined petroleum products and petroleum gases, including natural gas. Does not cover the entirety of HS Chapter 27, notably coal and other mineral fuels outside these headings. Non-hydrocarbon trade refers to goods outside this defined basket, rather than the exclusion of all energy-related products.
Green energy-related products 5 HS2012 six-digit codes (850231, 854140, 220720, 290919, 382600), mapped from Eurostat's three green energy product groups: wind turbines, solar panels and liquid biofuels. Covers wind-powered generating sets and products associated with photovoltaic technologies and liquid biofuels. The basket is an approximation because the underlying data are available at six-digit level, whereas some Eurostat product definitions use eight-digit codes. HS 854140 includes photovoltaic cells, LEDs and other photosensitive devices; HS 290919 covers ETBE alongside other ethers, including fossil-derived MTBE; and HS 220720 includes denatured ethanol for non-fuel uses. The basket therefore captures products beyond the intended green energy categories, while excluding other renewable energy technologies. Reported shares should not be interpreted as representing exclusively renewable energy products or the wider clean energy market.
High-technology products Project-specific HS2012 crosswalk derived from Eurostat’s SITC Rev. 4 high-technology aggregation: 301 six-digit codes across nine product groups. Covers aerospace, armaments, chemistry, computers and office machinery, electrical machinery, electronics and telecommunications, non-electrical machinery, pharmaceuticals and scientific instruments. The classification is based on product categories associated with high R&D intensity, rather than the technological sophistication of individual traded goods. The selected Pharmacy group is not equivalent to every pharmaceutical product, while the Armament customs group is not a comprehensive record of arms transfers, military deliveries or defence dependence. Trade flows do not directly measure domestic innovation capacity, technological ownership or R&D activity.
Agri-food HS Chapters 01-24 (Sections I-IV), following the Eurostat agricultural products definition used in the project. Covers live animals and animal products, vegetable products, fats and oils, prepared foodstuffs, beverages and tobacco. Includes products not intended directly for human consumption, such as animal feed and tobacco. Excludes agricultural inputs classified elsewhere, notably fertilisers.
Cereals HS Chapter 10. Covers wheat, rice, maize, barley and other cereals. A subset of the agri-food basket, analysed separately because of its relevance to food security and import dependence. Does not include cereal flours or prepared cereal-based food products classified under other chapters.
Fertilisers HS Chapter 31 (23 six-digit lines in the project trade workbook). Covers fertiliser products, including organic, mineral and chemical fertilisers and relevant mixtures. Analysed separately from agri-food because fertilisers fall outside HS Chapters 01–24. Does not capture the full range of fertiliser inputs or related chemicals classified under other chapters.

The baskets are defined for analytical purposes and are not mutually exclusive. Cereals form part of agri-food; HS854140 appears in both the green energy-related and high-technology baskets. Their values should therefore not be added together. Trade values and shares refer to the products classified under the specified HS codes, rather than the full markets or activities they are intended to approximate.

Interpretation and data limitations

Trade values may shift with commodity prices as well as volumes; this is especially important when interpreting hydrocarbon balances in the context of the 2022 energy price shock. Declining unit prices for some technologies also mean that import values do not necessarily track equipment quantities. For goods traded through intermediary hubs, the recorded counterpart need not be the final consumer or original producer. Reporting discontinuities, particularly those affecting recorded trade involving Iran and Russia in 2024, make it difficult to determine the extent of any underlying market contraction. Supplier concentration may indicate potential dependence, but is not in itself proof of coercive leverage or lack of alternatives.

Foreign direct investment

Database and variables

FDI data is drawn from the IMF’s Direct Investment Positions by Counterpart Economy, release 12.0.1, for 2019–2024. The variable used is inward direct investment positions, net (liabilities less assets), covering all instruments and entities, and measured at year-end in current USD. A bilateral position is attributed to the immediate counterpart economy, which may be a holding company jurisdiction rather than the location of the ultimate beneficial investor. This matters particularly for financial and treaty-routing centres, including Mauritius.

These figures represent accumulated positions, not annual inflows. Changes in FDI stocks may reflect acquisitions, divestment, reinvested earnings, exchange-rate and valuation movements, corporate restructurings and changes in data coverage. Separately cited World Bank balance-of-payments net FDI inflow figures provide context on recent financing flows and should not be confused with the counterpart-position data on FDI stocks.

Source preference and missing observations

For every host economy, counterpart and year, the host's official inward observation is used where available; otherwise a counterpart-derived (mirror) observation is used. IMF donor and host label variants are standardised before matching. The EU27 and GCC aggregates are constructed country by country and are subject to the same missing-data rules; overlapping regional counterpart aggregates are not added.

A bilateral position is averaged over the years in which it is observed within each three-year period. Missing observations are neither set to zero nor carried forward. Recorded zeros and negative positions are retained. Official world totals remain distinct from bilateral-position coverage; where world totals are counterpart-derived, missing bilateral records reduce the calculated total. The 'Other' category may include unselected counterparts and positions not attributed to a named counterpart, including negative residuals arising from differences in coverage.

Africa-specific concentration and GDP weighting

The African analysis supplements continent-wide stock shares with two measures for 2022–2024: geographic concentration and GDP-weighted investor share. Concentration is the proportion of an external actor's recorded African position held in its three largest host economies, with Mauritius identified separately when relevant. For host-level counts and shares of leading investors, a counterpart must be observed in each year of the period (for EU27 and GCC, at least one member in each year); Sudan and South Sudan are excluded where negative positions make shares unsuitable for comparison.

The GDP-weighted measure first divides each investor's recorded position in an economy by that economy's total recorded inward FDI stock. These country-level shares are then weighted by each host's share of the relevant economies' combined GDP, using average GDP in current USD for 2022–2024 from the World Bank World Development Indicators. The revised calculation covers 51 African economies. It measures relative presence in host economies of differing economic size; it does not represent the investor's share of African GDP, the importance of FDI for GDP or a direct indicator of leverage. Bilateral reporting gaps mean the resulting values are based on observed positions and may understate an actor's full presence.

ODA, other official flows (OOF) and Chinese commitments

OECD data and monetary measures

The analysis uses OECD DAC2A (ODA disbursements), DAC2B (other official flows OOF) and DAC3A (ODA commitments) datasets, covering the period 2019–2024. All values are expressed in constant 2024 USD. The principal ODA variable is net ODA disbursements (DAC2A measure 206). ODA commitments are taken separately from DAC3A measure 305; they record commitments, not funds disbursed during the period.

The project's gross official finance measure is constructed from ODA grants (201), gross ODA loan disbursements (204), OOF grants (2201) and gross OOF loan disbursements (2204). OOF equity (2217) is excluded. Gross grant/loan shares therefore reflect disbursed flows before loan repayments, not outstanding debt or the concessionality of loan terms. The constructed total is not identical to the OECD's narrower 'official development finance' indicator. For both ODA and OOF, commitments and disbursements are recorded separately, as they capture different stages of financing rather than separate financing flows. Adding them together would risk double-counting.

Both OECD comparison periods contain three years, so the analysis reports cumulative 2019–2021 and 2022–2024 totals. This differs from the annual averages used for most merchandise trade and debt-flow comparisons. Negative reported net flows remain negative. Missing reporting is not converted to zero, estimated or carried forward.

Donors and geography

In this section 'Europe' refers to the 27 EU Member States plus EU institutions, including the Commission and European Investment Bank (donor code 4EU001). The United Kingdom is treated separately. Mutually exclusive donor groupings prevent double counting of individual sovereign donors, 'Other bilateral', EU institutions, other multilateral institutions and overlapping aggregate categories. Private-donor series are not added to the official-provider universe. Provider rankings are restricted to the selected reporting donors specified.

For Africa, the aggregate benchmark is the OECD Africa recipient aggregate (F) less the country-coded Saint Helena series, applied to each donor and series. It retains regional and unspecified financing and is not reconstructed by summing individual recipients. The separate geographical analysis covers 53 country-coded African recipients; its five subregions need not add to the Africa-wide aggregate because geographically unallocated funds are absent from the country layer.

For the Middle East, the aggregate used in the final chapter is the OECD Middle East recipient aggregate minus country-coded Iran, retaining regional and unspecified allocations. It is distinct from both the 13-economy trade grouping and the six-economy low-/middle-income country analysis. Country comparisons focus on Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine*, Syria and Yemen where observations exist. The GCC and Israel are not recipients of ODA and thus appear in the analysis only as donors.

Saudi Arabia reports Africa-wide and Middle East-wide totals without comparable recipient-country allocations. Its totals are included where available at the regional level but its country and subregional entries remain blank, not zero. Its reported breakdown by grants and loans covers its available ODA flows; the OECD DAC2B extract contains no Saudi OOF observations. Some other actors, including India and Russia, are not comprehensively represented in the reporting and cannot be assumed to provide no finance. Country rankings describe recorded country-coded allocations, not necessarily complete donor portfolios.

AidData and China

Chinese official finance commitments are analysed separately using AidData’s China's Loans and Grants to Low- and Middle-Income Countries (CLG-LMIC) dataset, version 1.0. The project uses recommended adjusted commitment values for 2019–2023 in constant 2023 USD, distinguishing between ODA-like, OOF-like and unclassified commitments. Adjustments limit double counting of overlapping financing, rollover and refinancing arrangements. Africa-wide project figures include country-allocated projects and omit separately coded Africa-regional commitments, unless otherwise specified. The Middle East analysis also identifies documented collateral and repayment security arrangements where available.

AidData commitments are not added to OECD disbursement totals, nor directly ranked against them. They measure different events, cover different years and have different classification and price conventions.

Interpretation

Reported ODA can capture costs incurred in a donor country or elsewhere rather than resources transferred to a recipient's treasury. The coverage of OECD reporting donors, country allocations, grants and loans varies. The grant/loan split measures instruments disbursed, not donor influence, loan profitability or project performance. Missing country allocations particularly limit conclusions about the geographic distribution of Saudi finance and the relative rank of providers in individual recipient economies.

External debt

IDS coverage and creditor categories

The World Bank International Debt Statistics (IDS) is the principal source for external-debt stocks and associated lending flows. Its borrower coverage is narrower than that of the trade or FDI samples. The Middle East analysis covers the five reporting low-/middle-income borrowers Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria and Yemen; Palestine* is not in the debt sample due to lack of data. The African analysis covers 50 reporting economies, excluding Libya, Namibia, Seychelles and South Sudan due to lack of data.

Total external debt comprises a broader liability category than public and publicly guaranteed (PPG) external debt. Creditor-specific analysis is concentrated on reported PPG debt, distinguishing official bilateral creditors, multilateral creditors and private creditors according to IDS definitions. Individual EU Member States' bilateral claims are kept apart from EIB and other European institutional claims classified under multilateral debt. The GCC aggregate combines the six states' recorded official bilateral creditor positions; it is not a measure of all Gulf-origin finance, including finance provided through private or off-budget channels.

Stocks, flows and concessional debt

Outstanding debt stocks are compared at end-2019 and end-2024, in current USD; they are not three-year averages. Debt commitments, disbursements, principal repayments, interest payments and other annual flow measures are compared through average annual values for 2019–2021 and 2022–2024. The introductory section on African finance additionally reports cumulative net transfers for its two three-year periods: these cumulative values must not be inadvertently described as annual averages.

Net debt flows equal loan disbursements minus principal repayments. Debt service equals principal repayments plus interest. Net transfers equal disbursements less both principal and interest (equivalently, net flows less interest). A negative net-transfer figure indicates that debt-service payments exceeded new disbursements during the stated window. These indicators capture financing direction and servicing pressure, not necessarily changes in the outstanding nominal stock, which may also reflect currency movements, write-offs, restructuring and stock adjustments.

Concessional debt follows the IDS criterion of an original grant element of at least 35%. Creditor concessional shares are calculated against their relevant recorded bilateral PPG stock at the reference date. They are shares of outstanding claims, not a measure of new loans' terms. Where concessionality records are missing, reported shares are minimum recorded estimates rather than strictly comparable full-portfolio averages. The share can increase because non-concessional claims are repaid faster, without any increase in new concessional lending.

Reporting and interpretation

IDS may omit or incompletely report off-budget liabilities, politically contested obligations, debt owed by state-linked entities not captured in PPG, certain military-related arrangements and other undisclosed claims. Syria's and Yemen's largely frozen or discontinued data series limit interpretation of creditor movements. Lebanon's sovereign default complicates the relationship between outstanding liabilities and current debt-service payments. In Africa, the UAE-Egypt reporting break materially affects certain bilateral stock comparisons. The relative size of a creditor's recorded stock is therefore distinguished from new lending, net repayments, refinancing and debt restructuring. Rankings relate only to the specified creditor category and reporting borrowers.

Infrastructure

External engagement in infrastructure in the Middle East and Africa is examined through two World Bank sources whose indicators measure different activities and do not represent shares of a common infrastructure market.

The Private Participation in Infrastructure (PPI) database is used to select arrangements recorded as active that reached financial closure in 2000–2024, including closures before 2019. Because the extract lacks a stable project identifier, arrangements are deduplicated by host country, project name and year of financial closure; 61 of the resulting 70 project proxies in the Middle East and 565 of 722 in Africa have a foreign sponsor. The indicator is the share of attributed foreign sponsor stakes, unweighted by project value. Reported sponsor percentages are retained; where they are missing, the unreported stake is divided equally among the named sponsors without a percentage. Domestic, unassigned and multilateral stakes, including those of the International Finance Corporation (a member of the World Bank Group), are excluded from the foreign denominator, and attribution follows the sponsor’s recorded country rather than its ultimate owner. The measure includes management and lease contracts and state-owned sponsors; it does not measure investment value or verified current ownership. ‘Active’ refers to the source status, and does not confirm that a project is currently operational.

The Contract Awards in Investment Project Financing (IPF) dataset is used to examine contracts signed from July 2019 to December 2024 under World Bank-financed projects. The broad infrastructure-related sample covers contracts awarded under transport, water and sanitation, energy and extractives, and information and communication projects, plus all contracts classified under the ‘Works’ category; it therefore includes goods and services as well as construction. Actor shares are calculated using the value of contracts awarded to identified non-domestic actors: USD 401.5 million in the Middle East (USD 209.8 million in Works) and USD 14.7 billion in Africa (USD 10.9 billion in Works). Suppliers are attributed to their country of registration, which need not reflect ownership; subcontracting is not observed, and award values do not measure financing from the supplier’s home country. World-coded awards, mainly to UN agencies, form a separate category, while domestic and unassigned recipients are excluded from the denominator.

PPI refers to the host country while procurement data refers to the borrower country. The Middle East sample covers Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine*, Syria and Yemen, with qualifying investment project financing (IPF) awards only in the first four; the Gulf states and Israel appear only as external actors. The Africa sample covers 52 countries; awards to multi-country (regional) borrowers, worth USD 4.8 billion, are excluded because they cannot be assigned to a single borrower. In both regions, offshore registrations form a separate group.

Both indicators rest on samples concentrated in a few countries and contracts, and they differ in role, period and denominator. Their percentages should not be compared as matched shares of investment or ownership, and the absence of a record does not establish the absence of activity.

Arms transfers

Data on arms deliveries comes from the SIPRI Arms Transfers Database (trade register) and covers the period 2016–2025. Transfers are measured in SIPRI tend-indicator values (TIV, millions), which reflect the military capability of the weapons transferred rather than their financial value, and are expressed in millions. The dataset includes 1 349 transfers from 43 suppliers to 73 recipients in Africa, the Middle East and the Gulf. The suppliers are the 27 EU Member States (shown individually), the EU category (which includes only transfers SIPRI attributes explicitly to the EU and is not an aggregate of Member States), and the other actors in the report. Recipients are grouped according to the report’s regional classification, and non-country recipients such as the African Union, the Libyan House of Representatives and the RSF are kept separate. Gaza and the West Bank are mapped to Palestine*. A transfer is included if at least one of its listed delivery years falls within the period, so the data measures deliveries, not orders. Where a transfer is delivered over several years, its TIV is divided equally across all listed delivery years, and only the shares falling within 2016–2025 enter the totals. Annual values for multiyear transfers are therefore estimates, not observed SIPRI figures. Variations are calculated between 2018–2021 and 2022–2025.

Military bases

Data on foreign military bases come from the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), The Military Balance 2026, and reflect the situation in 2025. Bases associated with EU Common and Security Defence Policy (CSDP) military missions have been added to reflect missions currently deployed as reported by the EU (https://www.eeas.europa.eu/eeas/missions-and-operations_en#87694). The indicator counts bases and facilities, not personnel, equipment or the size or function of each site, so a large permanent installation and a small logistics hub count equally, such as the US base of Camp Simba at Manda Bay (Kenya). Troop deployments, multinational operations and missions without a dedicated base are not counted, so the figures do not consider military presence through temporary or host-nation facilities.

Military deployments

Data on military deployments comes from the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), The Military Balance 2026, and reflects the situation in 2025. The dataset records, for each external actor and each African, Middle Eastern or Gulf country (or maritime area, such as the Red Sea), the personnel deployed, by type of deployment. Four types are distinguished. The first comprises bilateral deployments, i.e. forces stationed or deployed within the framework of a bilateral agreement. The second covers personnel in UN peacekeeping and observer missions (e.g. MINUSCA, MONUSCO, UNMISS, UNIFIL, UNDOF, UNTSO). The third covers EU missions and operations (e.g. EUTM Somalia, EUTM RCA, EUMAM Mozambique, Atalanta, Aspides), plus NATO’s Mission in Iraq. The fourth encompasses other multilateral operations, such as Operation Inherent Resolve, whose operations in Iraq terminated on 30 September 2026, and the Multinational Force and Observers (MFO) in the Sinai. Estimates for Russian private military companies are recorded separately. The indicator counts personnel deployed, not units or equipment. Deployments by EU Member States are shown individually and aggregated for the EU total.

Military exercises

Data on military exercises covers the period from 2024 to September 2026 and is drawn from a manually compiled database based on official and open sources, including releases from defence ministries and armed forces, such as AFRICOM, the Indian Press Information Bureau, the Saudi Press Agency, EUNAVFOR communications and specialised defence media. Information on earlier editions is scattered across many sources and languages and is difficult to locate and verify through manual compilation, so the dataset begins in 2024.

For each exercise, the database records the name, year, type (naval, air, land, special forces or interforce), area of operations, and the lead and co-lead countries. For this study, figures for land and land special forces, such as counterterrorism exercises, are aggregated. An exercise is attributed to the external actor(s) that lead or co-lead it; participating countries are not systematically analysed due to inconsistencies in reporting and only mentioned when participation could be verified through at least one official source. Operations linked to ongoing conflicts are excluded. The indicator counts exercises, not their scale, duration or troop numbers, and covers only publicly reported activity.

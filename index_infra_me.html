Anchoring ties: infrastructure and construction

  • Gulf sponsors have the largest share of attributed foreign sponsor participation in the selected World Bank Private Participation in Infrastructure (PPI) sample, followed by the EU. Both positions are concentrated in Jordan, and the portfolio largely reflects projects reaching financial closure before 2019.
  • Turkish-registered suppliers lead the selected World Bank infrastructure-related procurement sample, overwhelmingly in Iraq.

Infrastructure development across the Middle East and the Gulf combines large programmes of new construction with substantial needs to repair and rebuild existing assets.

In the GCC, the annual average value of contracts awarded more than doubled between 2019–2021 and 2022–2024, from USD 105.8 billion to USD 212.4 billion. Saudi Arabia and the UAE’s combined share rose from 70.7% to 84.4%, indicating greater concentration in these two markets.(1)

Reconstruction presents a different set of needs across conflict-affected economies. In Syria, rebuilding is a central challenge following the fall of the Assad regime in December 2024. A World Bank assessment released in 2025 estimated the cost of reconstructing physical assets damaged during 2011–2024 at USD 216 billion, including USD 82 billion for infrastructure. In Gaza, the joint World Bank–EU–UN assessment published in April 2026 estimated recovery and reconstruction needs at USD 71.4 billion, based on damage assessed through 9 October 2025. In Lebanon, the World Bank’s March 2025 assessment of the 2023–2024 conflict estimated USD 11 billion in recovery and reconstruction needs across ten sectors. This included approximately USD 1 billion requiring public financing for infrastructure sectors. In Yemen, renewed Saudi-Houthi fighting in 2026 risks adding to reconstruction needs, already estimated at USD 20-25 billion in 2020.

While these assessments cover different periods, places and sectors, from physical reconstruction to broader economic and social recovery, taken together they indicate the scale of rebuilding needs (although should not be combined into a current regional infrastructure financing gap).

But finance is only one way in which external actors contribute to shaping infrastructure across Middle East and the Gulf. Foreign companies also build, equip, sponsor and operate projects. The first part of analysis that follows therefore examines two specific dimensions: contracts awarded under World Bank-financed projects and private infrastructure projects reaching financial close in the World Bank’s Private Participation in Infrastructure database. Neither dataset covers the entire market, but together they offer comparable evidence on who contributes to building infrastructure and who participates as private project sponsors across low- and middle-income economies in the region.

Sponsorship and procurement in low- and middle-income economies(2)

World Bank-financed infrastructure-related procurement

Turkish-registered suppliers led the selected broad infrastructure-related portfolio with 33.1% of the non-domestic total(3).

Other considered actors accounted for significant smaller shares. Gulf suppliers received 5.3%, the EU27 3.7%, the United States 2.5% and China 2.4%. These shares describe the covered awards, rather than national positions in the region’s construction market.

The narrower works sample comprises USD 209.8 million in non-domestic awards across only 12 World Bank contract numbers. This represents 25.3% of all works-award value in the selected portfolio. Türkiye’s share rises to 44.0% in this small sample.(4)

Private participation in infrastructure

Gulf sponsors account for 24.5% of attributed foreign sponsor stakes, ahead of the EU27 at 19.3%.(5)

Other African and Middle Eastern economies represent 12.2%, the United States 7.2% and South Korea 6.9%.

At 13.2%, the UAE contributes just over half of the Gulf total. China’s 0.9% share comes from participation in one Jordanian power project.

The portfolio largely reflects earlier financial-closure decisions: 95.7% foreign-linked proxies closed before 2019; only three reached financial closure in 2019–2024. This is not a measure of new investment over that period.

Geographic and contract concentration

The PPI rankings depend heavily on Jordan, which hosts 40 of the 61 foreign-linked proxies and accounts for 65.5% of attributed foreign sponsor stakes. Jordan represents 85.5% of the EU’s own sponsor measure and 76.1% of the Gulf’s.

Procurement, in turn, is concentrated in Iraq, which accounts for 81.7% of broad non-domestic award value and 91.6% of non-domestic works-award value. Iraq represents 98.1% of Türkiye’s broad award value and 96.7% of the Gulf’s.(6)

The evidence identifies distinct, highly concentrated forms of infrastructure participation. Gulf and European sponsors feature prominently in an established PPI portfolio centred on Jordan, while Turkish-registered suppliers lead the selected World Bank procurement sample, overwhelmingly in Iraq. A few large contracts strongly shape this result. The EU’s relatively substantial sponsor position coexists with a much smaller procurement share. China’s recorded shares are small in these particular samples (they do not establish its overall infrastructure presence across the Middle East and the Gulf).

These commercial relationships can provide channels for influence. The datasets do not show whether sponsors or suppliers convert them into political leverage, control over assets or enduring dependence. The findings are strongest as evidence of specific commercial positions within the covered portfolios. Differences between the two measures should not be read as shares of one regional market or as a shift over time.

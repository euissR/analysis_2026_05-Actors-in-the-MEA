Levers of influence

Europe has an unusually broad and deep-rooted presence across Africa, and the Middle East and the Gulf, although its position differs between the two regions. In Africa, it remains an incumbent heavyweight even as other actors’ involvement grows. In the Middle East and the Gulf, it is one of several leading partners, alongside China in trade, the US in security and Türkiye in diplomacy. In an increasingly competitive geopolitical environment, Europe’s challenge is less to expand this presence than to leverage it better.

Three patterns

  1. Europe’s scale is collective. EU institutions and Member States bring complementary assets that together give Europe a reach no other single actor matches, from diplomatic and cultural networks to trade, investment and development finance. Individual Member States retain significant strengths in selected fields, but it is collectively that Europe operates on the scale of the largest external actors across multiple dimensions. These assets, however, are not always joined up effectively.
  2. Europe’s overall position is broadly resilient, but some of its advantages are narrowing. It holds its economic ground in trade, but its shares of recorded investment and its aid have fallen in both regions, while in some strategic sectors such as green energy products other actors are moving faster. Beyond economics, leadership is increasingly distributed: the EU records the most high-level visits to Africa, but China has the widest diplomatic network there; in the Middle East and the Gulf, Türkiye has the broadest diplomatic footprint and the US remains the main security partner.
  3. Presence does not automatically translate into favourable perceptions or political alignment. Europe’s extensive institutional, economic and people-to-people ties do not give it a comparable advantage in public opinion: in the African countries surveyed, China’s influence was viewed more positively than the EU.

Main findings

Levers of Influence maps how the EU and selected external actors(1) engage through diplomacy, soft power, economic ties and military cooperation. It identifies where Europe remains strong, where its position is coming under pressure, and where interests converge.

The landscape of external partnerships across Africa, the Middle East and the Gulf is becoming more plural, but no new hierarchy of influence is emerging. No actor leads across diplomacy, soft power, trade, finance, infrastructure and security, and advantages in one domain do not necessarily carry over to others.

The EU retains substantial weight across both regions, but that weight translates unevenly into political alignment, favourable perceptions and sectoral advantage. Its comparative advantage lies in the breadth of its relationships rather than dominance in every domain – and that breadth is collective. Taken together, EU institutions and Member States combine economic, diplomatic, cultural and security ties on a scale that none could sustain alone across both regions. In Africa, the EU remains the leading trading partner, largest recorded FDI counterpart and largest provider of official finance among the actors tracked. But these strengths are uneven: its trade lead is heavily anchored in North Africa, while China leads across sub-Saharan Africa, and EU Member States contribute far fewer personnel to UN peacekeeping than India or China. In the Middle East and the Gulf, Europe remains the largest recorded FDI counterpart and a major commercial and development partner, but leadership is distributed: China leads goods trade, Türkiye has the widest diplomatic footprint and the US remains the main security partner. European net ODA has also declined in both regions. The EU therefore retains exceptional reach, but increasingly operates in fields where other actors hold stronger sectoral or regional positions.

China combines unusually broad commercial and diplomatic strengths. It has embassies in, and formalised partnerships with, all 53 African states it recognises, it is the largest trading partner of the Middle East and the Gulf, and supplies three-quarters of Africa’s green-energy imports. Its position is nevertheless uneven across instruments. Recorded Chinese FDI in Africa remains far below EU’s, and although China is the continent’s largest official bilateral creditor, repayments exceeded new lending in 2022–24. In Africa, however, China is also expanding its security presence, not only through its long-established military base in Djibouti but also through military exercises with Egypt and countries in the western Indian Ocean region.

The United States has a more security-centred profile, particularly in the Middle East and the Gulf, where it remains the main security partner, largest arms supplier and, in 2025, maintained the largest military deployment. Its economic role is more mixed: it was the second-largest aid provider to Africa among the actors examined in 2022–24 and retains important investment positions, but accounts for under 5% of Africa’s trade and has a relatively small, largely legacy bilateral creditor position.

The Gulf states have increasingly prominent but differentiated roles as investors, donors and security partners. Their recorded investment in Africa rose by around 40%, driven largely by the UAE, while Saudi Arabia is a major, predominantly grant-based donor. In Africa, Gulf countries complement their diplomatic outreach with a much wider network of development and humanitarian projects. Within the Gulf, regional governments are also taking a more active role in organising security cooperation with multiple external partners. Israel has a distinct profile: it is the region’s only net exporter of high-tech goods, while its security relationships in Africa have expanded, with recent arms transfers concentrated mainly in Morocco.

Türkiye, India, Japan and South Korea have distinct strengths. Türkiye combines the widest diplomatic network in the Middle East and the Gulf with extensive educational, migration, development and security ties both in Africa and the Middle East and the Gulf. India’s overall trade share in Africa is relatively modest, but it plays an outsized role in cereal and selected pharmaceutical supplies and deploys more UN peacekeepers there than any other actor examined. Japan stands out for continued lending and a near-doubling of its aid in the Middle East. South Korea has a smaller financial footprint but a highly concessional lending portfolio, while its arms transfers to the Gulf (mainly to the UAE) rose sharply between 2018–21 and 2022–25.

Russia and Iran have more concentrated official profiles. Russia’s recorded arms transfers to Africa fell by more than four-fifths between the two comparison periods, while its military presence is concentrated in a small number of countries, increasingly through Africa Corps. Its economic footprint is also limited, although it occupies important niches: despite accounting for less than 1% of Africa’s trade, Russia supplies around 12% of its cereal imports. At the same time, Russia has maintained and expanded its diplomacy and soft power outreach, notably through the Russia-Africa summits, Russian Houses and development projects. Iran’s engagement is narrower but has also expanded in selected areas. Its cultural presence is concentrated particularly in West and East Africa, through the Islamic Culture and Communication Organization. Its security engagement has also grown, including arms transfers to East Africa in recent years.

Across the analysis, the nature and concentration of a relationship can matter as much as its overall scale. India accounts for only around 6% of Africa’s total trade but supplies about 14% of its cereal and selected pharmaceutical imports while engaging with the regions both through soft power and military cooperation – in particular through exercises in the Indian Ocean. In the lower- and middle-income Middle East, Türkiye supplies nearly a quarter of agri-food imports despite not being the largest overall trading partner. The same distinction appears in finance: the largest creditor is not necessarily the largest source of new lending. Aggregate rankings can therefore obscure relationships that are particularly important to individual countries or sectors, and through which dependencies and potential leverage may arise.

Partner countries actively shape these relationships. Governments in both regions combine partnerships with different external actors rather than aligning consistently with any one of them across economic, diplomatic and security domains. At the UN, alignment varies sharply by issue: abstention on Ukraine remains widespread, while African states and most Middle Eastern and Gulf states have aligned with the EU’s average position on Gaza roughly four times in five since 2023. Public attitudes are similarly complex. In the African countries surveyed by Afrobarometer, 62% of respondents view China’s influence positively, compared with 50% for the EU; in Iraq and Lebanon, largely negative views of the US nevertheless coexist with a preference for stronger economic ties.

For the EU, the challenge is therefore less to expand its presence across the board than to use its existing weight more selectively and coherently. This means acting collectively where scale matters, concentrating effort around partners’ priorities, turning existing relationships into tangible benefits and making those results visible, and helping partners make greater use of access to the European market. The objective is not to match every external actor in every field, but to identify where Europe’s collective assets offer distinctive value and deploy them accordingly.

Four priorities

Taken together, the findings point to four priorities:

  • Act collectively, especially where scale matters, not least in strategically important sectors where others are moving faster. Combine EU instruments with Member States’ bilateral strengths and pool resources through Team Europe where this can increase impact, rather than pursuing parallel initiatives.
  • Communicate what Europe delivers. Coordinate communication across EU institutions and Member States, and make it clear, concrete and tailored to each country, for governments, local communities and civil society alike. This matters most where results are visible locally, such as jobs, infrastructure and skills, and for the standards that distinguish Europe’s approach – transparency, accountability, and labour and environmental protection – even where they make delivery slower.
  • Engage on partners’ priorities. Support demands for greater representation in multilateral institutions, seek common ground issue by issue, and build on converging security interests, from maritime security off Africa’s coasts to the transition after UNIFIL in Lebanon, bearing in mind that partners will continue to diversify their relationships and will not align with the EU across the board.
  • Build on Europe’s most stable asset, trade, by making access to the European market easier to use. Maintain EU standards on labour rights, environmental protection and transparency, while providing practical support to help partner governments and businesses meet them and realise the benefits of closer trade.

References

1. The selected external actors are the EU, the United States, China, Russia, India, Japan, South Korea, Türkiye, Iran, Israel and the six Gulf Cooperation Council states (Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates). They form a purposive, non-exhaustive sample of actors both relevant for the EU and for countries in the region because of a substantial or strategically significant engagement across several of the dimensions examined in Africa and/or the Middle East and the Gulf. The selection also reflects the availability of sufficiently comparable data across indicators and countries. It should therefore not be read as a ranking of the most influential external actors or as comprehensive coverage of all countries active in either region.

The Gulf states and Israel are analysed both as part of the Middle East and the Gulf and, where relevant, as external actors because they also project significant economic, diplomatic, financial or security engagement beyond their borders, including in Africa. Iran is treated only as an external actor. Engagement among African states falls outside the scope of this project, although some indicators capture intra-regional activity.

Africa covers 54 economies grouped into five project subregions: North Africa; West Africa; Central Africa; East Africa and the Horn of Africa; and Southern Africa and the Indian Ocean. The Middle East and Gulf comprises 13 economies: GCC countries, Israel, Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine*, Syria and Yemen. For details, see the methodological notes.

* This designation shall not be construed as recognition of a State of Palestine and is without prejudice to the individual positions of the Member States on this issue

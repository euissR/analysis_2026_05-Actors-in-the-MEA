Anchoring ties: infrastructure and construction

  • Private infrastructure sponsorship (covered by the World Bank database) is relatively diverse: EU sponsors have the largest footprint, but in most host countries infrastructure sponsorship is shared among several foreign actors.
  • Construction awards are far more concentrated: Chinese-registered firms accounted for 71% of the value of contracts awarded to non-domestic firms in the selected World Bank-financed portfolio and lead in 27 of 42 African countries with such awards.
  • The geography also differs: Europe’s sponsor presence is broad, while Chinese construction is especially strong in East Africa and the Horn.

Africa’s infrastructure needs remain substantial. The African Union Commission and the OECD estimate that the continent must invest around USD 155 billion a year until 2040 – almost twice the 2016–20 annual average – to reach infrastructure levels comparable with similar economies. Yet shrinking fiscal space makes this harder. As the previous section shows, Africa’s external debt has risen, while net debt transfers shifted from a sizeable inflow in 2019–21 to an outflow in 2022–24. African governments funded 41% of infrastructure investment in 2016–20, while bilateral and multilateral development partners provided another 48%. Yet fiscal constraints were substantial: in 2019–23, governments spent on average seven times more on debt service than on infrastructure. External financiers thus play a major role, although their roles are evolving.

Among key actors, the EU pursued infrastructure investment through several channels, including the Global Gateway Africa-Europe Investment Package, mobilising by end 2025 nearly €120 billion towards a target of €150 billion by the end of 2027. China, which financed African infrastructure extensively through loans in the 2010s but has since entered a period of net financial outflows, shifted its focus towards FDI and construction contracts. In 2024 alone, Chinese Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) construction engagement across Africa amounted to USD 29.2 billion. The Gulf states also became increasingly important actors in Africa’s infrastructure landscape.

But finance is only one way in which external actors contribute to shaping African infrastructure. Foreign companies also build, equip, sponsor and operate projects. The analysis that follows therefore examines two specific dimensions: contracts awarded under World Bank-financed projects and private infrastructure projects reaching financial close, as recorded in the World Bank’s Private Participation in Infrastructure database(1). Neither dataset covers the entire market, but together they offer comparable evidence on who contributes to building Africa’s infrastructure and who participates as private project sponsors.

World Bank-financed infrastructure-related procurement

Across World Bank-financed infrastructure procurement in Africa, China dominated among foreign suppliers. Chinese-registered firms captured 58.7% of identified non-domestic award value, compared with 12.1% for EU firms.

The contrast was even stronger in construction works: China accounted for 70.9% of non-domestic award value, against 8.0% for the EU.**

These continental figures mask a clear geographic divide. European suppliers perform much better in North Africa, where they accounted for 79.8% of the value of contracts awarded to foreign firms, while China’s advantage is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, where Chinese-registered companies secured 74.2% of the value of construction contracts awarded to foreign firms.

Private Participation in Infrastructure

The picture is very different for Private Participation in Infrastructure (PPI). Across active PPI arrangements** in Africa, EU sponsors accounted for 34.3% of the foreign sponsor-stake measure, well ahead of other African and Middle Eastern sponsors at 17.9%. The UAE alone accounted for 7.5% (about three-quarters of the Gulf total of 10%), the US for 7.0% and China for just 2.7%*.

Europe therefore held the largest attributed foreign sponsor share in this PPI sample, while China’s position was much stronger in the separate construction awards sample. This points to different roles within the recorded infrastructure activity***.

The geography of infrastructure engagement also differs by instrument and actor. PPI sponsor participation is relatively dispersed across the continent,

while construction awards are more concentrated, particularly in East Africa and the Horn, which together account for one third of the total value of construction works- contracts awarded to foreign firms.

Actor patterns reinforce the contrast. The EU’s participation in PPI is spread across 39 countries, led by South Africa, Egypt and Morocco,

but its construction contract awards are much more concentrated in North Africa, which accounts for 48.1% of their total value, with a single Egyptian railway contract accounting for 39.6%.

China exhibits a markedly different pattern: its PPI footprint is small and present in only 12 countries,

while its construction activity spans 35 and is especially strong in East Africa, with Tanzania, Ethiopia and Kenya together accounting for 40.6% of the total value of its construction works contracts. Its position is also dominant within several individual borrower countries: Chinese-registered firms accounted for more than 90% of foreign works-award value in Angola, Botswana, Ethiopia, Kenya, Madagascar and Tanzania.

Some smaller external actors are even more geographically concentrated: UAE works awards are almost entirely confined to Nigeria and Niger, while US works awards in this sample are confined to Somalia and Ethiopia.

The analysed data on infrastructure engagement in Africa reveals a contrast between private project sponsorship and construction contracts. Among active arrangements reaching financial closure in 2000–2024 covered by the World Bank’s Private Participation in Infrastructure database, EU sponsors accounted for 34.3% of attributed foreign sponsor stakes, compared with just 2.7% for China. These shares measure the relative participation of identified foreign sponsors, not investment value, financing or asset ownership. Europe’s sponsor presence was also geographically broader, extending across 39 countries compared with China’s 12.

The picture is different in World Bank-financed infrastructure procurement. Chinese-registered firms captured 58.7% of identified non-domestic award value and 70.9% of foreign construction works awards in July 2019-December 2024, compared with 12.1% and 8.0% respectively for EU firms. The geography also differed considerably. European construction awards were concentrated in North Africa, while Chinese firms had a much stronger presence in sub-Saharan Africa, especially East Africa and the Horn. Chinese firms accounted for the largest share offoreign works awards in 27 of the 42 African countries covered by that comparison.

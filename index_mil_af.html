Securing interests: military engagement

This section analyses military cooperation through four layers: arms transfers, military bases, deployments and joint exercises. Most of the external actors considered are present across these layers, but with varying intensity and geographical focus.

The EU, the US, China, India and Türkiye stand out for the breadth of their engagement with the continent, but none plays an overall leading role.

The presence of so many actors adds to the complexity of the military landscape. On the one hand, it reflects African countries’ agency in diversifying their partnerships. On the other, where external interests diverge, it increases the risk of competition and tensions spilling over, particularly when closer partnerships intersect with regional or global security fault lines.

For the EU, this means operating in a crowded environment across the continent, especially in the Gulf of Guinea and the Gulf of Aden and western Indian Ocean, where joint exercises are concentrated.

Arms transfers

Between 2016 and 2025, the US, Russia and China were the largest suppliers of arms to African countries. The top five suppliers also include France, Italy and Germany.

Among the top providers, China supplied weapons to the widest number of countries in Africa (32).

Egypt is the main recipients of arms transfers accounting alone for 31% of registered arms transfers to the continent.

Morocco received 56% of US arms transfers to the continent and 66% of Israeli arms transfers, while Algeria received 27% of Chinese arms transfers and 60% of Russian arms transfers.

Since 2022, Morocco also received the biggest share of Israeli’s transfers to African countries.

Overall, comparing 2016–2021 with 2022–2026, seven countries increased their arms transfers to African countries: China (+15%), France (+214%), Germany (+303%), Iran (+367%), Israel (+775%), Türkiye (+78%) and the US (+129%).

However, the increase varied across African regions. China increased its transfers mainly to West Africa; France, Germany and the US to North Africa; Iran to East Africa; Israel to North and East Africa; and Türkiye to West and East Africa.

Focusing on sub-Saharan Africa alone, the picture changes.

China results the first supplier of arms to sub-Saharan countries, followed by Russia, France, and Türkiye.

While Chinese transfers are widespread, the Russian ones are more concentrated in some countries such as Ethiopia, Mali, and Uganda.

In sub-Saharan Africa, Nigeria received the largest volume of arms transfers, mostly from China and Türkiye.

Türkiye, China and Iran supply armed drones to different countries. Türkiye has the most widespread network of clients, while Iran has transferred drones only to Ethiopia and Sudan respectively in 2021 and 2023.

Military bases

Military bases are strongly concentrated in Djibouti (US, China, France, Italy and Japan). However, the US also uses lighter footprint presence in the region, such as Camp Simba, a cooperative security location near Manda Bay, Kenya, where US and Kenyan forces cooperate for regional security.

Deployments

However, the established presence of military bases alone only provides a partial reading of foreign military presence in Africa. Therefore, a look at foreign deployments provides a more comprehensive picture.

The largest bilateral deployments in Africa are associated with military bases, particularly in Djibouti, where the US has 4,000 personnel deployed, France 1,500 and China 400. Türkiye’s military base in Somalia also hosts 800 personnel.

Some EU Member states maintain smaller deployments where bilateral agreements have established bilateral mission. Thus, France maintains small deployments in Cote d’Ivoire (80 personnel), Gabon (100), and Senegal (250) while Italy deploys bilateral missions in Libya (160 personnel) and Niger (250).

Different from traditional deployments, it is the case of Russian private military companies (PMCs) – now mostly absorbed by Africa Corps – that are deployed mostly in the Sahel countries, Libya and the Central African Republic. Russian PMCs reportedly have large deployments, particularly in Libya (approximately 3,500 personnel), Mali (approximately 1,500) and the Central African Republic (approximately 1,500).

Several external actors tracked also deploy military personnel to the UN peacekeeping missions on the continent.

In 2025, the US and Russia had very limited deployments to UN missions, respectively 18 and 24 personnel overall, while EU Member States deployed 267 personnel, with Portugal alone deploying 215 personnel to MINUSCA in the Central African Republic.

In contrast, India was the largest contributor to UN missions in Africa among the considered actors. In 2025, it deployed 4081 personnel overall with the biggest deployments to MONUSCO in the DRC (1,076 personnel) and UNMISS in South Sudan (2,401), where China also deploys a large contingent (1,053).

Chinese deployments in 2025 were the second largest among the considered countries with overall 1360 personnel of which 1053 to UNMISS in South Sudan and 285 to UNISFA, Abyei*.

Military exercises

Military exercises add another layer to understanding the extent and depth of military ties. They show how closely external actors work with regional forces. The Gulf of Guinea and the Gulf of Aden/Western Indian Ocean stand out as particularly crowded maritime areas, where exercises concentrate.

Between January 2024 and July 2026, we registered 55 joint military exercises between external partners and African countries. About 52% of these exercises included navies or a maritime security component. 26% were exercises interforces and 7% air forces and 11% land or special forces.

15 exercises were US-led, including multilateral exercises such as African Lion and Obangame express to which EU member states also participate.

China co-organised four exercises: two air forces exercises with Egypt (Eagles of civilisation), one naval with South Africa and other BRICS countries (Will for Peace), and one with Mozambique and Tanzania (Peace and Unity).

Russia run a joint bilateral exercise with Egypt in 2025 involving naval and air forces (Bridge of friendship).

In 2025, India also launched the Africa-India Key Maritime Engagement (Aikeyme), a naval exercise in the Indian Ocean to which several African coastal states participated (Tanzania, Comoros, Djibouti, Kenya, Madagascar, Mauritius, Mozambique, Seychelles, South Africa). In addition, India held bilateral exercises with Egypt and Seychelles and a trilateral one with South Africa and Brazil (IBSAMAR VIII).

The EU and its Member States organised about 29 joint exercises in Africa or in its continental waters.

EUNAVFOR ATALANTA continued its joint exercises with African coastal states, such as Djibouti, Kenya and Seychelles, and expanded its international cooperation through joint exercises with India and Japan. EUNAVFOR ASPIDES also launched joint exercises with Djibouti and some Passex exercises.

Among EU Member States, France co-organised the largest number of exercises with Morocco, Senegal, Gabon and countries in the Gulf of Guinea and the Yaoundé architecture. Also, Spain co-organised seven naval exercises mainly within the framework of the Coordinated Maritime Presence in the Gulf of Guinea.

Shaping markets: trade and commercial ties

  • The EU remains Africa’s largest trading partner, but its strength is concentrated in North Africa. China leads in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Trade balances reveal diverging trajectories. Between 2019-21 and 2022-24, the EU shifted from an overall trade surplus to a deficit, largely due to higher energy costs, while China expanded its surplus.
  • China strengthened its dominance in green-energy products and narrowed the gap with the EU in high-tech goods. India, Türkiye and Gulf countries played more selective but significant roles, highlighting an increasingly diversified commercial landscape.

Trade lies at the core of economic relations between states. It connects governments through trade agreements and regulatory frameworks, firms through supply chains and commercial relationships, and societies through the goods and services they buy and sell. It is also the most comprehensively measurable dimension of these ties: unlike investment or aid, trade is recorded for almost every actor and partner in the region(1).

Examining the scale and composition of these exchanges therefore offers a first indication of how closely economies are connected, where dependencies are emerging and which external actors matter most to everyday economic activity. Trade does not automatically translate into political influence. But being the main buyer of a country’s exports, or its main supplier of goods that are difficult to source elsewhere – such as energy, staple foodstuffs or critical technology – can give a trading partner leverage that aggregate trade volumes alone do not reveal.

Consequently, this section looks not only at who trades most with African countries, but also at where dependence is concentrated and in which direction it runs.

Trading partners

The EU remained Africa’s largest single trading partner, well ahead of China and marginally behind the group of Asian countries considered (China, India, Japan and South Korea), which between 2022-24 accounted for 28.7% of Africa’s trade on average.

However, the continental picture concealed substantial regional variation.

The EU's position rested on North Africa, which generated over half of its trade with Africa in 2022–24. Similarly, the EU accounted for close to half of North African trade, almost five times China's share.

Across Sub-Saharan Africa, the ranking reversed: China accounted for nearly a quarter of trade exchange, against the EU's nearly one-fifth.

China's strongest position was in Central Africa, where its share rose from 33.8% to 39.0% - the largest gain by any actor considered across the five regions - and became more than twice the EU's.

Trade balances

Those different positions are reflected not only in how much each actor traded with Africa, but in the role they played within that trade. The energy-price shock after Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, made this especially visible: the EU emerged as a major buyer of African energy, while China consolidated its role as Africa’s largest net supplier of goods.

The EU moved from an average annual surplus of USD 22.7 billion in 2019–21 to a USD 15.2 billion deficit in 2022–24

as its hydrocarbon deficit nearly doubled to USD 62.3 bn;

but even excluding hydrocarbons, its surplus fell by USD 8.6bn, to USD 47.1bn.

China moved in the opposite direction: its overall surplus rose by USD 26.1bn to USD 73.0bn,

while its non-hydrocarbon surplus reached USD 101.1bn, more than twice the EU's.

The contrast was sharpest in North Africa, which accounted for almost 70% of the EU's continent-wide swing:

there the EU, with nearly five times China's trade share, ran an average annual deficit of USD 12.7bn, while China recorded a USD 31.9bn surplus.

Selected product groups

The 2022 hydrocarbon shock shows why aggregate trade patterns tell only part of the story. A partner that accounts for a modest share of total trade can still wield considerable leverage if it dominates the supply of critical goods or markets for key exports. The next section therefore shifts the focus from who trades most with Africa to who matters most in selected product groups relevant to the future of energy security and energy transition (green energy products(2)), food security (agricultural goods(3)), and competitiveness and technological sovereignty (high-tech products(4)).

Energy security increasingly depends on access to clean-energy technologies, not just fuels - and China was becoming Africa’s dominant supplier. Africa’s imports of green-energy products remained small but nearly doubled between the two periods to USD 2.2bn.

China’s share reached three quarters of Africa’s imports, while the EU’s fell by more than half to less than a tenth.

Even in North Africa, the EU’s strongest trading region, China supplied two thirds of green-energy imports, compared with less than one quarter from the EU.

High-tech trade presents a more balanced picture. The EU remained the largest supplier, with its share broadly stable at 30.2%, but China closed much of the gap between the two.

Their strengths lay in different segments: the EU retained an edge in specialised products such as pharmaceuticals and aircrafts,

while China led in high-volume electronics and telecommunications equipment, on which digital connectivity increasingly depends.

India illustrates why aggregate trade values can obscure sector-specific importance. Although it accounted for only 6.2% of Africa’s total trade,

India supplied 14.3% of imports in the selected high-tech pharmaceutical basket in 2022–24, second only to the EU among the actors considered.

Its role was particularly pronounced in sub-Saharan Africa: it accounted for around a fifth and more than a quarter of pharmaceutical imports in West and Central Africa and in East Africa and the Horn respectively.

Agriculture*, in turn, exemplifies a more two-way trade relationship, with external actors mattering both as exporters to African markets and as importers of African exports.

The EU stands out in this respect: in 2022–24 it absorbed close to one third of Africa’s agricultural exports

while supplying nearly one quarter of its agricultural imports.

The EU absorbed 53% of Central Africa’s agricultural exports in 2022–24, compared with 39% in West and North Africa.

Yet it received only 19% of Central Africa’s goods exports overall.

Agriculture thus stands out as a particular EU commercial strength in a subregion that accounts for less than 5% of EU–Africa goods trade.

China showed the opposite geography, playing only a marginal role in former’s agricultural trade but a larger, though still modest in SSA.

Cereals matter more than their share of agricultural trade suggests. They account for around half of Africa’s calorie intake,(5) making their suppliers particularly important.

Among the actors considered, the EU mattered most, supplying 26.9% of cereal imports in 2022–24.

However, two partners with much smaller shares of Africa’s overall trade also played outsized roles. India, which accounted for around 6% of Africa’s total trade, supplied 14.4% of its cereal imports in 2022–24, up from 10.0% in the previous period*.

Russia, despite accounting for less than 1% of Africa’s overall trade, supplied a further 12.2%. Its share fell sharply in 2022 following the disruption of Black Sea trade, but recovered to 14.9% by 2024.

Fertilizers* reveal an even sharper geographical divide in Africa’s trade with external actors.

North Africa generated 83% of Africa’s fertilizer exports in 2022–24, including 96% of its exports to the EU and 93% to India.

Among the actors tracked, the EU was the largest market for African fertilizers, absorbing 18.8% of exports, followed by India (9.3%) and the US (6.3%).

Sub-Saharan Africa presented almost the opposite picture, accounting for 87% of the continent’s fertilizer imports.

Its suppliers were more diversified: Gulf countries supplied 11.7% of imports, Russia 10.5% and the EU 9.4%.

By contrast, the EU alone supplied 36.4% of North Africa’s fertilizer imports, highlighting its much stronger two-way trade relationship with that subregion.

The EU remains Africa’s largest trading partner, although its position is heavily anchored in North Africa, where it accounts for close to half of all goods trade. China, by contrast, leads in sub-Saharan Africa, accounting for nearly a quarter of total goods trade against just under a fifth for the EU. Moreover, between 2019–21 and 2022–24, the EU’s trade surplus with Africa turned into a deficit, largely reflecting a widening hydrocarbon deficit, while China further expanded its substantial goods trade surplus.

Other partners play outsized roles in selected goods important to health and food security. India accounts for around 6% of Africa’s total goods trade but supplies approximately 14% each of its pharmaceutical and cereal imports. Russia accounts for less than 1% of overall trade but supplies 12% of cereal imports. In sub-Saharan Africa, Russia and the GCC collectively each account for a larger share of fertiliser imports than the EU.

Overall, the EU’s share of Africa’s goods trade has remained relatively stable, but this masks important differences and shifts across regions and sectors, as well as the growing importance of other actors in specific markets.

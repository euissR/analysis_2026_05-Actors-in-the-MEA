Shaping Perceptions: cooperation, people and cultural reach

  • Development cooperation activities are concentrated in the Middle East, where the EU has three European Investment Bank (EIB) offices and its Member States have a total of 13 development agency offices, with Germany’s GIZ having the largest network among them. However, Türkiye’s TİKA has wider geographical coverage, with offices in five countries and Palestine*. In the Gulf, only Germany’s GIZ (in Saudi Arabia and the UAE) and Japan’s JICA (in Saudi Arabia) have field offices.
  • EU Member States lead in cultural and language outreach, with a total of 54 institutes, including 32 French institutes and 10 Goethe-Institut offices. Türkiye’s Yunus Emre Institute has 13 institutes across the region, followed by China’s Confucius Institutes with 10 and Russia with eight cultural centres.
  • Türkiye hosts the largest number of students from the region (98 820) and the largest migrant population (4.15 million), mostly from Syria and Iraq. The EU ranks second in student numbers (66 704), while Saudi Arabia ranks second in migrant population (2.9 million), largely from Yemen. In the opposite direction, India has the largest migrant population in the region (8.3 million), concentrated in the Gulf, while the EU’s registered migrant population (370 000) is concentrated mainly in Israel. Among the three largest destinations for migrants from the region, the EU recorded the fastest growth (+144 %).

Soft power refers to the ability to influence others through persuasion or attraction and thus complements other forms of power that rely more on coercion, such as military power or economic pressure.1 This publication does not engage with the doctrinal debate on the concept. It understands soft power as the capacity of countries or entities to exert some power of attraction or persuasion towards their own culture, ideas and preferences and a favourable image of itself. It is not exercised exclusively by states per se, as other actors including foundations and institutes, can also contribute to it.2 This analysis, however, focuses on entities that are financially or institutionally closely associated with the states considered. In many instances, soft power engagement expands the geographical and diplomatic outreach through other means such as scholarships, language institutes or development cooperation projects. Its inclusion in this study provides a fuller picture of the external actors’ outreach to countries and peoples in the Middle East and the Gulf.

This section provides an overview of some of the factors that contribute to the development of soft power and its geographical reach. It begins by examining the geographical presence of development cooperation and humanitarian aid activities by country. As each external actor pursues development cooperation through different approaches, the analysis considers several indicators of geographical presence: the existence of local offices, which represents the highest degree of institutional presence; the implementation of projects; and the identification of priority countries in national strategies. Forms of cooperation also vary, ranging from traditional development projects to grants. Given the diversity of actors and approaches, it was not possible to assess both geographical presence and financial commitments consistently across all actors. The analysis therefore focuses exclusively on geographical presence.

The section then examines cultural cooperation, focusing on the presence of language and cultural institutes established by external actors. Finally, it explores opportunities for developing people-to-people ties through three indicators: the number of international students from the region studying in each of the external actors' countries; the size of Middle Eastern and Gulf diasporas in those countries; and the size of external actors' diasporas in Middle Eastern and Gulf countries. Taken individually, these indicators cannot provide a comprehensive measure of people-to-people ties or foreign policy alignment. Together, however, they help identify patterns of proximity between countries in the region and external actors, as well as grassroots connections that can foster mutual understanding.

Geographical distribution of development agencies, grants and projects in the Middle East and the Gulf

The Middle East has the highest concentration of development branches among the external actors examined.

EU Member States have a total of 13 offices across four countries (Jordan, Lebanon, Iraq and Israel) and Palestine*. The development agencies of five Member States – France, Germany, Italy, Portugal and Spain – also have offices in the region.3 GIZ has the largest network, with four field offices in the Middle East (Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon and Palestine*) and two in the Gulf (Saudi Arabia and the UAE). In addition to EU Delegations, the EU has three offices of the European Investment Bank (EIB) in Jerusalem, covering the West Bank and Gaza, as well as in Jordan and Lebanon.

The Japanese development cooperation agency (JICA)4 is the only other actor examined with a field office in the Gulf, located in Saudi Arabia.

However, the Abu Dhabi Fund for Development (ADFD) reports projects in Bahrain and Oman,

while the King Salman Humanitarian Aid and Relief Centre (KSrelief) reports projects in Oman.5

In the Middle East, the Turkish development agency (TIKA)6 has the widest office network, with a presence in five countries (Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria and Yemen) and Palestine*.

JICA has offices in four countries (Iraq, Jordan, Syria and Yemen) and Palestine*.

However, when projects are also considered, Russia, Qatar, the UAE, and Saudi Arabia are active in several countries across the region. Rossotrudnichestvo7 reports projects in Palestine*, Syria, Lebanon and Jordan.

The Qatar Fund for Development and the Abu Dhabi Fund for Development report projects throughout the region, except in Israel,

while the US International Development Finance Corporation8 reports projects only in Jordan.

Cultural cooperation and language institutes

Cultural cooperation and language institutes present locally also create a fertile environment for favouring mutual knowledge and understanding. Thus, it represents an important component of the power of attraction that external actors can have in the Middle Easte and the Gulf.

Eight EU Member States have 54 cultural and language institutes in four Middle Eastern countries and Palestine, as well as across all Gulf countries.

France has the largest network, with 21 French institutes and 11 Alliance française offices, while Germany has 10 Goethe-Institut offices across the region.

Italy and Spain both have cultural institutes in Israel and Lebanon, with Italy maintaining a third institute in the UAE and Spain in Jordan.

The Czech Republic has two cultural offices in Israel, while Hungary and Poland each have one cultural institute in Israel, and Finland has one in Lebanon.9

Türkiye has the second largest network with three Yunus Emre Institutes10 in the Gulf (Bahrain, Qatar and Oman) and 10 in the Middle East, including five in Syria, two in Palestine* and one each in Iraq, Jordan and Lebanon.

Russia has eight cultural centres overall, including Russkiy Mir Foundation centres in Jordan and Lebanon, and five Russian Houses in Israel, Palestine*, Jordan, Syria and the UAE. Notably, Iran hosts four offices of the Russkiy Mir Foundation, although it is considered only an external actor in this publication.11

Among Asian actors, China has the largest presence, with 10 Confucius Institutes across the UAE, Palestine*, Bahrain, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, Israel and Jordan.

South Korea has one Korean Cultural Centre in the UAE, four Sejong Institutes in the Gulf and two in the Middle East,

while India's only cultural centre in the region is in Israel.12

Gulf countries rely less on cultural institutes than on cultural missions, attachés and offices. Saudi Arabia and Oman have six each, Bahrain five and Kuwait four.

These are located mainly in other Gulf states, as well as in Jordan and, in Saudi Arabia’s case, Lebanon.13

Students from the Middle East and the Gulf in external actors14

People-to-people ties are also favoured by studies and living abroad. The following section therefore examines the presence of students from the Middle East and the Gulf in the external actors considered.

In 2023, Türkiye hosted the largest number of students from the Middle East and the Gulf (98 820), of whom 59 % were from Syria and 16 % from Iraq.

The EU ranked second among the actors examined, hosting 66 704 students from the region, of whom 31 % were from Syria and 25 % from Lebanon.

In 2022, Iran hosted 66 227 students from the region, almost exclusively from Iraq (91 %).

In 2023, the US hosted considerably fewer students (34 582), of whom 47 % were from Saudi Arabia and 17 % from Kuwait.

In 2024, Saudi Arabia hosted 34 415 students from the region, of whom 48 % were from Yemen, 24 % from Syria and 13 % from Palestine*.

Recorded migrant stocks from the Middle East and the Gulf in the external actors considered15

Migrants also help foster people-to-people ties. We therefore analyse migrant stocks in both directions: from the Middle East and the Gulf to external actors, and vice versa.

In 2024, Türkiye hosted the largest number of migrants from the region, particularly the Middle East, with a total of 4 million, including 3.6 million from Syria and 0.4 million from Iraq.

Saudi Arabia hosted the second-largest migrant population from the region, with approximately 2.9 million people, including 2 million from Yemen, 0.5 million from Syria and 0.2 million from Jordan.

In the same year, the EU ranked third, hosting approximately 2.4 million migrants from the region, including 1.6 million from Syria and 0.6 million from Iraq.

Among these three actors, the EU recorded the largest percentage increase in its migrant population from the region between 2015 and 2024 (+144 %), compared with 80 % in Türkiye and 10 % in Saudi Arabia.

However, Japan recorded the largest percentage increase among all the actors examined, with its migrant population from the region tripling between 2015 and 2024, albeit from a low base (from 482 to 1 451).

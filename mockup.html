Economy

Comparing 2019–21 with 2022–24, China gained +1.7pp of the region's world trade, the biggest gain of any actor (followed by two other Asian countries, India and South Korea). Collectively, China, India, Japan and South Korea increased their combined share from 35.0% to 38.1% between the two periods and accounted for 41.3% of regional trade in 2024. Between 2019-2021 and 2022-2024, these four actors accounted for nearly half (46.7%) of the increase in average annual regional trade value.

The United States is commercially less dynamic; it remains highly important for Israel and selected strategic sectors, but overall regionally between 2022-2024 was in the same league as Japan and South Korea when it comes to share of regional trade (5-7%). The Gulf states are also significant trading partners in their own right: driven by UAE and Saudi Arabia, between 2022-2024 intra-Gulf trade averaged 12.3% of the Gulf’s world trade. Following signing of the Abraham Accords, UAE-driven trade with Israel rose from nearly zero in 2019 to $2.9bn by 2024 (noteworthy, but still a rounding error [0.2%] in the Gulf's overall trade). Toward the middle- and low-income Middle East the Gulf is a consistent net supplier, accounting for 1.3% of its total trade between 2022-2024. Türkiye is geographically concentrated in low and medium-income countries of the Middle East, where like the Gulf it is a net supplier.

On the headline balance (*an actor's total goods surplus with the region, all products, actor's perspective) EU leads: from +$58.1bn/yr (2019–21) to +$39.7bn (2022–24), recovering to +$58.3bn by 2024. The dip was the energy bill post 2022 Russia’s invasion of Ukraine**. But EU’s large non-hydrocarbon surplus more than offsets that deficit, and grew from +$82.4bn to +$90.8bn/yr.

The very diversification the Gulf is pursuing deepens a new external dependency. The Gulf states are using hydrocarbon revenue, earned from a balanced field of buyers, to fund energy transitions whose hardware comes almost entirely from one supplier. On the export side, hydrocarbons (~$521bn/yr in 2022–24) stay spread across China (24.6%), the wider Asian bloc of China, India, Japan and Korea (62.7% combined), and the EU (10.8%, up 2.1pp as Europe substituted Gulf and Iraqi crude for Russian supply).

On the import side the picture inverts: China's share of green-energy equipment rose from 55.1% in 2019-201 to 68.5% in 2022-204, while the EU's fell 8.5% to 3.9%, driven above all by solar (China accounts for ~91% of the region's solar import). The market remains small, about $4bn/yr between 2022-2024, under 1% of hydrocarbon exports - but its strategic importance is forward-looking, as concentrated solar supply could increasingly affect costs and project timelines. This largely reflects a global structure: China holds around 85% of solar supply-chain capacity.

Average annual imports of high-tech products, as defined by Eurostat, rose by 75%, from $2.75bn in 2019–2021 to $4.81bn in 2022–2024. China captured most of this expansion: its share more than doubled from 16.6% to 35.7%, while EU27’s fell from 32.9% to 20.7%. Saudi Arabia drove much of the shift, increasing its share of the regional market from 23.1% to 34.3%; within the Saudi market, China’s share climbed from 18.5% to 41.4%, while EU27’s dropped from 32.2% to 16.7%. Unlike solar equipment, however, the high-tech market remains more diversified. EU27’s average annual sales still rose from $905m to $995m, leaving it a substantial second supplier with continued strengths in specialised products and selected country markets, even as China became the market leader.

Agri-food is one of EU’s most resilient commercial positions in the region, although its strength is uneven across product categories. It remains key supplier, with its share broadly stable at 17.9% in 2019–2021 and 17.3% in 2022–2024, while average annual sales rose from $14.4bn to $17.5bn. China remained a marginal supplier, while the more significant gains came from India (8.1% to 9.4%) and Türkiye (7.7% to 8.6%).

Finance

Importance of various external financial flows on high income versus low- and middle—income economies in the region

Who appears on the FDI ownership ledger, and what the data miss

The EU is the Middle East’s largest recorded FDI counterpart. It accounted for roughly one-third of inward stock in the high-income economies in 2024 and nearly half in the lower-income group, although Saudi Arabia’s reporting change makes the 2024 high-income figure only indicative. Temporary shifts in Jordan’s total stock also distort the lower-income shares, while the value of European investment remained broadly stable.

The United States ranks second in the high-income group and is the only major counterpart whose position rose every year, driven mainly by a 75% increase in US-linked investment in Israel. European investment in Israel also increased in value, but its share declined as Israel’s total inward stock expanded sharply. China’s position there fell.

Excluding Türkiye, whose USD 49.1 billion in recorded ODA consists overwhelmingly of Syria-related refugee-hosting costs incurred within the country, official finance to Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine, Syria, and Yemen declined between 2019–2021 and 2022–2024. The contraction was sharper in commitments than in actual payments, suggesting that existing programmes sustained disbursement levels even as the pipeline of new financing weakened.

Official finance shows who provides support, through which instruments and on what terms. Debt turns the perspective around: it shows who already holds a claim, who is being repaid, and where creditor exposure may translate into leverage when refinancing, restructuring or repayment pressures emerge. The two layers are connected: the loan component of official finance can become debt stock once it is disbursed and remains outstanding. But loans are a small share of official finance in this sample, so most donor support creates no debt obligation at all. Debt data therefore reveal a different set of relationships: accumulated lending over time, the cost of servicing it, and creditors that may be invisible in the aid statistics because they do not report there, because their claims predate the period, or because they operate through different channels.

Within official bilateral public and publicly guaranteed external debt, Japan is the leading and most clearly expanding state-creditor actor. It has the largest bilateral stock, positive net flows in both 2019–2021 and 2022–2024, continued commitments, and the highest concessionality among major bilateral creditors. EU27 member states remain large but are in run-down mode. China is modest, declining, and records no new PPG commitments during 2019–2024. The US is a legacy repayment creditor: its bilateral stock falls rapidly, while its apparent rise in concessionality is largely a denominator effect as non-concessional claims are repaid faster than concessional ones.

Security

The US has been the main arms provider to the region, especially to Saudi Arabia and Israel, followed by the EU, especially to Qatar. Both providers registered a peak in 2022 with a decrease in 2024, but while the EU drastically reduced transfers in 2025, the US increased them again. In 2019, Russia provided arms to Iraq and Syria, but in 2024 only provided air systems to Iran. Israel provided missiles and munitions to the UAE in 2022 (the only country to which Israel transfers arms in the region).

The US has the largest presence in the Middle east with several bases across the region. However, multiple other countries have deployments in the region within the framework of UN peacekeeping missions, NATO missions, especially in Iraq, and the coalition against daesh.

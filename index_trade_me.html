Shaping markets: trade and commercial ties

Trade lies at the core of economic relations between states. It connects governments through trade agreements and regulatory frameworks, firms through supply chains and commercial relationships, and societies through the goods and services they buy and sell. It is also the most comprehensively measurable dimension of these ties: unlike investment or aid, trade is recorded for almost every actor and partner in the region(1).

Examining the scale and composition of these exchanges therefore offers a first indication of how closely economies are connected, where dependencies are emerging and which external actors matter most to everyday economic activity. Trade does not automatically translate into political influence. But being the main buyer of a country’s exports, or its main supplier of goods that are difficult to source elsewhere – such as energy, staple foodstuffs or critical technology – can give a trading partner leverage that aggregate trade volumes alone do not reveal.

Consequently, this section looks not only at who trades most with countries across Middle East and the Gulf(2), but also at where dependence is concentrated and in which direction it runs.

Trading partners

The EU remains a major and resilient partner, but it is not gaining ground.

It continues to run the largest overall goods surplus with the region, underpinned by a large and growing non-hydrocarbon surplus.

China recorded a modest increase in its share of regional trade – the largest among the actors tracked. Together with the other Asian actors tracked (India, Japan and South Korea) it accounted for 41.3% of regional trade by 2024.

The continental picture conceals substantial regional variation. High-income economies accounted for 86.6% of average annual trade in 2022–24.

They are also more deeply integrated into global markets: China is the largest partner; the EU27 and the US are major suppliers, intra-GCC trade is significant, and India, Japan and South Korea are important buyers of Gulf hydrocarbons.

The middle- and lower-income Arab economies have smaller and more fragmented trade relationships. China still leads in total trade, and by a wider margin, with the EU as the second-largest partner, but India, Türkiye and the US carry more weight, while Iraq’s oil exports heavily shape the group’s external balances.

Trade balances

Trade balances reveal distinct actor positions. EU remained the region’s largest overall net supplier, with an average annual surplus of USD 39.7bn in 2022–2024.

Türkiye strengthened its position as a net supplier, with its surplus rising from USD 14.9bn to USD 20.2bn per year between the two periods.

China, despite remaining a net importer overall in 2022–2024 because of its hydrocarbon purchases, saw its average annual non-hydrocarbon surplus rising sharply to USD 115.3bn, well above the EU’s USD 90.8bn.

Further differences re visible between the two income groups. EU and the US were net suppliers to the high-income economies, but both ran deficits with the lower- and middle-income group, with Iraq accounting for all of the EU deficit and most of the US one.

Iraq was also pivotal for Türkiye, but in the opposite direction. Türkiye surplus with lower- and middle-income economies was nearly six times larger than the one with high-income group, with more than two-thirds generated in Iraq.

Selected product groups

The weight of hydrocarbons in Middle Eastern trade - and the extent to which the 2022 energy-price shock reshaped trade balances - shows why aggregate trade shares tell only part of the story. Commercial importance depends not only on how much actors trade with the region, but also on what they buy and sell, and how concentrated alternative suppliers or markets are. A partner that accounts for a modest share of total trade can still wield considerable leverage if it dominates the supply of critical goods or markets for key exports. The next section therefore shifts the focus from who trades most with Middle East and the Gulf to who matters most in selected product groups relevant to the region’s energy transition and economic diversification (green energy products(3)), food security (agricultural goods(4)), and competitiveness and technological sovereignty (high-tech products(5)).

China has become the region’s dominant supplier of green-energy equipment, while the EU has lost substantial ground.

Among the high-income economies, China’s share rose from 56.6% in 2019–21 to 69.6% in 2022–24, while the EU’s fell to 4.3%.

The shift is particularly pronounced in the Gulf, where shift towards renewable-energy is part of broader economic-diversification strategies. Israel is the main exception, retaining a much stronger European supplier position.

China also dominates in the middle- and lower-income economies, although these markets are smaller and more fragmented and regional suppliers retain a larger role. The EU’s position is particularly weak, accounting for only 1.1% of their green-energy equipment imports in 2022–2024.

This concentration is becoming more consequential as demand expands. Regional imports were 2.7 times larger in 2022–24 than in 2019–21, reaching USD 12.1bn over the three-year period.

High-tech trade is more contested. The EU remains the region's largest external partner, counting both imports and exports, and China has only just caught up as a supplier.

But that lead rests on Israel, the region's only net high-tech exporter and the one economy with a strongly European orientation.

Excluding Israel, China leads.

Sectoral positions reveal niche strengths: the US supplied 55.8% of the high-tech armament basket against 13.3% of high-tech imports overall,

while India held 10.8% of pharmaceutical imports in middle- and lower-income economies

and South Korea 10.2% of regional high-tech armament imports.

Agri-food was one of the EU’s more resilient commercial positions in the Middle East and Gulf, where it was the largest supplier among the partners covered in 2022–24.

Beneath this aggregate picture, however, sourcing patterns varied sharply. Gulf countries drew on a relatively broad mix of global suppliers, while lower- and middle-income economies sourced nearly a quarter of their agri-food imports from Türkiye alone.

Israel showed a different form of concentration, sourcing 38% of its agri-food imports from the EU.

These relationships became more consequential as the region’s average annual agri-food deficit widened by 34%, reaching USD 72.6 billion in 2022–24.

Cereals, a regional staple, illustrate how unevenly exposure to individual suppliers was distributed. India remained an important source across both income groups despite restrictions on wheat exports from 2022 and on some rice categories from 2023.

Russia’s role, by contrast, was concentrated in the GCC and Israel, where it supplied around 14–16% of cereal imports in 2022–24, compared with around 2% in lower- and middle-income economies.

China has emerged as the Middle East and Gulf’s largest trading partner, ahead of the EU. Unlike in Africa, where China’s lead is concentrated in sub-Saharan economies, it leads across both income groups. Together with other Asian actors (India, Japan and South Korea), China accounted for 41.3% of regional trade in 2024.

At the same time, the EU retains considerable commercial weight. It remains the region’s largest overall net supplier of goods, with an average annual surplus of USD 39.7bn in 2022–24. China’s non-hydrocarbon surplus, however, was larger: USD 115.3bn against the EU’s USD 90.8bn. China has also consolidated its dominance in green-energy equipment, capturing a growing share of a rapidly expanding market as the EU loses ground.

Across other selected product groups, high-tech and agri-food, the picture is more picture differentiated. The EU remains the largest two-way high-tech trading partner, but its lead rests heavily on Israel; excluding Israel, China takes the lead. The EU is also the largest agri-food supplier among the partners tracked, although Türkiye plays a particularly important role in lower- and middle-income economies. India and Russia occupy significant niches in cereal supplies, while the US dominates the high-tech armament basket.

The EU’s commercial position therefore remains substantial, but its relative strength varies considerably across countries and sectors.

Shaping perceptions: cooperation, people and culture

  • Beyond diplomacy, external actors project considerable soft power across the region. In addition to the sections of the Commission Directorate-General for International partnerships (INTPA) within the EU Delegations, the European Investment Bank (EIB) has several regional offices in Africa, while EU Member States’ offices and partnerships further extend Team Europe’s presence across the continent.
  • The development agencies of Türkiye and Japan also have a significant network of field offices. By contrast, India and the Gulf countries mostly operate from headquarters in their respective countries, with the exceptions of Qatar Charity and the India Exim Bank.
  • Regarding people-to-people ties, the EU remains the primary destination for African students, followed by Türkiye and the US. Migrant stocks both from Africa to the EU and from the EU to Africa indicate that this is the most significant migration relationship among the external actors considered. However, the stock of African migrants Türkiye increased almost sixfold between 2015 and 2024 (+491%), albeit from a low base.

This analysis focuses on entities with close financial or institutional ties to the states considered. In many instances, soft power engagement expands these states’ geographical and diplomatic outreach through other means such as scholarships, language institutes or development cooperation projects. Including these initiatives in this analysis provides a fuller picture of external actors’ outreach to countries and peoples in Africa.

This section begins by examining the geographical distribution of development cooperation and humanitarian aid activities by country. As each external actor pursues development cooperation through different approaches, the analysis considers several indicators of geographical presence: the existence of local offices, which represents the highest degree of institutional presence; the implementation of projects; and the identification of priority countries in national strategies. Forms of cooperation also vary, ranging from traditional development projects to grants. Given the diversity of actors and approaches, it was not possible to assess both geographical presence and financial commitments consistently across all actors. The analysis therefore focuses exclusively on geographical presence.

The section then examines cultural cooperation, focusing on the presence of language and cultural institutes established by external actors. Finally, it explores opportunities for developing people-to-people ties through three indicators: the number of international students from Africa studying in each of the external actors’ countries; the size of African diasporas in those countries; and the size of external actors’ diasporas in Africa. Taken individually, these indicators cannot provide a comprehensive measure of people-to-people ties or foreign policy alignment. Together, however, they help identify patterns of proximity between countries in the region and external actors, as well as grassroots connections that can foster mutual understanding. Finally, the section provides an overview of perceptions of the influence of external actors in Africa.

Geographical distribution of development agencies, grants and projects in Africa

Western Africa registers the largest number of external actors’ development agency offices, 73 in total.

The EU has two regional offices of the European Investment Bank (EIB) in Côte d’Ivoire and Senegal with the mandate of financing projects in sustainable infrastructure, private sector development and climate mitigation(1). EU Member States’ agencies collectively maintain 45 field offices with GIZ (Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit) having the widest network, followed by France’s Agence française de développement (AFD), with 10 field offices.

Other external actors are present in West Africa with 26 field office overall.

The Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA), and Qatar Charity have field offices in eight countries each,

while the Turkish Cooperation and Coordination Agency (TİKA) has five field offices in the region.

In Eastern Africa and the Horn, non-EU external actors have the same number of field offices as in West Africa (26).

However, the Turkish TİKA has the largest network with field offices in eight of the region’s nine countries.

JICA and Qatar Charity follow with seven and six field offices respectively.

The EU and Member States’ development institutions are also widely present in the nine countries of East Africa and the Horn. The EIB has an office in Ethiopia covering both the country and the African Union, and another in Kenya covering East Africa.

EU Member States have 19 additional field offices, including seven operated by Germany, five by France and three by Italy.

In Southern Africa and the Indian Ocean, the EU and its Member States’ development branches are present in 12 out of the 14 countries in the region. In Eswatini, Mauritius and Seychelles partnerships are managed through the competent EU Delegations and the EIB regional office in South Africa.

Other external actors collectively maintain a presence in 10 of the region’s 14 countries.

Japan has the most extensive network, with nine field offices, while the presence through offices of other actors is limited.

However, as some of the external actors conduct their cooperation from their home country, if we consider grants, India covers 13 countries in the region also thanks to the presence of the India Exim Bank in South Africa.

In Central Africa, the EU and its Member States are present in all 10 countries.

Germany and France are present in seven countries, while the EIB has a regional office in Yaoundé, Cameroon.

Other actors have a more limited presence. Japan has the widest network among them, with field offices in just four countries.

However, when grants are taken into account, India’s development cooperation also extends to all ten countries,

while Gulf countries finance development projects in several countries in Central Africa through the Qatar Fund for Development, the Abu Dhabi Fund for Development and the King Salman Humanitarian Aid and Relief Centre, which operate from Qatar, the UAE and Saudi Arabia, respectively.

Russia’s Rossotrudnichestvo also conducts projects in Burundi, CAR, Republic of Congo, and the DRC.

In North Africa, the EU and Member States’ development branches are present in all five countries. The EIB has three offices in North Africa alone, located in Cairo, Rabat and Tunis, while the EU is the only external actor represented in every North African country with field offices.

The only other actor active in all five North African countries is the Abu Dhabi Fund for Development (ADFD)(2) but its presence is limited to projects.

Cultural cooperation and language institutes

Cultural cooperation, including language courses offered locally by several external actors through dedicated institutions, plays an important role in fostering mutual understanding. It therefore represents an important component of the power of attraction that external actors can promote in Africa.

EU Member States have cultural or language institutes in 50 African countries. The French cultural cooperation network spans 47 countries in Africa, with 124 Alliance française centres and 57 Institut français centres.

French cultural institutes are present in every country in Central Africa and, on average, in about 84% of countries across the other African regions. This average rises to 89% when cultural institutes from all EU Member States are considered(3).

China has also a large network of cultural institutes in Africa with 70 Confucius Institutes(4) and 11 Confucius classrooms present in 49 countries.

Yet Chinese regional coverage varies from full country coverage in Central Africa to 78% coverage in Eastern Africa and the Horn.

Russian cultural cooperation centres in Africa include Russian Houses managed by Rossotrudnichestvo and offices of the Russkiy Mir Foundation(5). They are present in 17 countries overall. However, Russian Houses have expanded their geographical presence through the establishment of at least eight other affiliated institutions run locally(6) and in September 2026, Rossotrudnichestvo announced the opening of eight additional Russian Houses in Africa(7).

The Turkish Yunus Emre Institutes(8) are present in 13 African countries overall

and North Africa is the only region where every country hosts at least one centre.

Presence of African students in external actors(9)

Another key component of soft power is people-to-people ties, which can develop through studies and living abroad. The following section therefore examines the presence of African students in the external actors considered.

In 2023, the EU, Türkiye, the US and Russia were the main destinations for African students, although the numbers varied considerably.

The EU hosted 272 804 students from Africa, accounting for 57% of the total number of African students in the external actors considered. France alone hosted about half of the overall EU figure.

Türkiye was the second-largest destination, hosting 59 668 students (13%), a figure comparable to that of the US (55 391, or 12%).

Most African students in the EU came from North and West Africa, followed by Central Africa.

Türkiye attracted the largest numbers of students from North Africa, East Africa and the Horn of Africa, while the US was the second-largest destination for students from West Africa, after the EU.

Approximately 5 000 students from West Africa studied in Russia and 2 500 in India.

In 2024, around 6 500 West African students were studying in Saudi Arabia, while in 2022, approximately 4 000 were studying in Iran.

Russia was the second most popular destination for North African students, with the largest share coming from Egypt. The third destination was Türkiye, with about half of the students coming from Egypt (9 595) and a quarter from Morocco (4 958).

Saudi Arabia and India also attracted a fair share of African students, although far fewer than the EU and other leading destinations. In 2024, Saudi Arabia hosted 18 095 African students, mainly originating from West Africa, North Africa and East Africa, particularly Nigeria, Egypt and Sudan.

In 2023, India hosted 11 042 African students, particularly from East and Southern Africa with Tanzania and Zimbabwe, respectively, as the main countries of origin.

Recorded migrant stocks from Africa in the external actors considered(10)

Migrant stocks are another important dimension of people-to-people ties. Migrants can build bridges between their host countries and communities of origin, contribute economically to their countries of origin through remittances and foster mutual understanding. This section examines the registered stocks of migrants from Africa in the countries of the external actors considered.

In 2024, the EU recorded the largest stock of migrants from Africa among the external actors considered, with approximately 9.3 million living in EU Member States.

The US ranked second, with a migrant stock from Africa of 1.8 million.

Türkiye ranked third, with 175 894 migrants from Africa, but recorded the largest percentage increase in its migrant stock since 2015 (+491%, from 29 757 to 175 894).

The largest shares of the EU’s African migrant stock originated in North Africa (5.7 million) and West Africa (1.6 million).

In the US, migrants from West and East Africa accounted for the largest shares of the African migrant stock (0.8 million and 0.6 million, respectively).

In Türkiye, East Africans represented the second-largest share (43 348), after North Africans (116,171).

Although the overall numbers were lower in China, migrants from West and Southern Africa accounted for the largest shares of its African migrant stock (11 233 and 10 682, respectively).

Recorded migrant stocks from the external actors considered to Africa(11)

Just as migrant stocks from Africa in the countries of external actors provide an indication of the extent of people-to-people ties, so too do migrant stocks from external actors in Africa. This section therefore examines migration in the opposite direction: from external actors to African countries.

In 2024, among the external actors considered, the EU accounted for the largest migrant stock in Africa, with a total of 307,920 people living on the continent.

China and India ranked second and third, with estimated migrant stocks of 97,417 and 85,832 people, respectively.

Russia followed with 41 020, and the US with 38 770.

The largest migrant stocks from the EU, China, Russia and the US are concentrated in North Africa, although in different countries.

Migrants from the EU are primarily registered in Morocco, those from China and Russia in Egypt, and those from the US in Libya.

In contrast, the largest concentration of migrants from India is found in Southern Africa and the Indian Ocean region, with 64 254 people, including 27 674 in South Africa, out of a total Indian migrant stock of 85 832 across Africa(12) .

In 2024, East Africa and the Horn of Africa accounted for the second-largest regional concentration of migrants from China in Africa, with 29 599 people, the largest migrant stock from any of the external actors considered in the region.

This stock was overwhelmingly concentrated in Ethiopia (26 824 people, or 90.6% of the subregional total).

However, the Chinese migrant stock in Ethiopia reportedly declined by approximately 51% between 2015 and 2024, from 54 628 to 26 824.

Perceptions of external actors’ influence(13)

Finally, surveys of public perceptions in Africa provide insights into how different external actors are viewed.

China is the external actor whose influence is perceived most positively in Africa, both on average (62 %)

and in every region.

Across the continent, the US ranks second (52 %), followed by the EU (50 %).

Former colonial powers (41 %), India (39 %) and Russia (36 %) receive lower ratings.

Conversely, former colonial powers are the actors whose influence is perceived most negatively on average (31 %), followed by Russia (23 %).

The US (20 %), India (20 %), the EU and China (17 %) receive lower negative ratings.

However, in North Africa, positive perceptions of US influence (35 %) are less widespread than those of EU influence (46 %) and only slightly more widespread than those of Russian influence (32 %).

In Central Africa, positive perceptions of the EU (49 %) are only slightly higher thanpositive perceptions of Russia (46 %).

In Eastern Africa, positive perceptions of the EU (45 %) are similar to positive perceptions of India (45 %), while Russia only scores 25%.

In Southern Africa, perceptions of Russia having a positive influence are the lowest among the actors considered (26 %).

In West Africa, Russia (44 %) and India (41 %) share approximately the same level of positive perception.

Overall, the EU retains a leading role in development cooperation and cultural outreach, with a few Member States, notably France and Germany, maintaining the largest networks. Together, the EU and its Member States have the broadest presence across the continent. Türkiye stands out as an actor that is expanding its soft power capacities, particularly in North and East Africa, while China’s presence is more evenly distributed across regions and sectors, except for East Africa, where a large but declining Chinese migrant population is concentrated in Ethiopia. However, the EU’s extensive outreach is not always matched by equally favourable perceptions. Closer coordination and planning among Member States and EU institutions, combined with stronger Team Europe branding, could make the EU’s presence and the results of its partnerships more visible.

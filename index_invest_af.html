Financing influence: investment, aid and debt

  • The EU retained the broadest financial footprint among actors considered, leading in aid and recorded investment positions, although the US narrowed its gap in the former in 2022-24.
  • Lending patterns are shifting: between 2019-21 and 2022-24, China remained Africa’s largest bilateral creditor despite net repayments, while multilateral finance increasingly replaced bilateral lending.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI) was highly concentrated: in roughly one-third of African economies, a single external actor accounted for over half of recorded inward FDI stock.

As with trade, external financial exposure does not translate directly into influence. What matters is not only how much money flows, but also through which channels, how concentrated those flows are and what kind of relationships they create. Foreign direct investment (FDI) creates long-term commercial interests by embedding foreign firms in local economies. Official development assistance (ODA) links donor countries to governments through financial support for budgets, services and infrastructure. Remittances create leverage by giving host countries the power to restrict migrants’ access to their labour markets.

At continental level, remittances(1) were the largest and most stable of the three flows in 2022-24 (totalling USD 287.6 bn); FDI ranked second, narrowly ahead of ODA (on account of exceptional inflows into Egypt). However, the balance between the three flows differed sharply by region. North Africa received only 8% of Africa’s ODA, but 44% of its remittances and 39% of its FDI. In sub-Saharan Africa, by contrast, ODA was the largest of the three flows, highlighting a different pattern of external financial exposure.

Debt adds a different form of exposure, tying borrowing countries to creditors through accumulated obligations as well as new financing. Across Africa(2), cumulative net transfers on external debt - new disbursements minus principal and interest payments – shifted from a USD 97.0 bn cumulative inflow in 2019–21 to a USD 7.5 billion outflow in 2022–24. North Africa accounted for just over half of this deterioration, led by Egypt, although net transfers also deteriorated across sub-Saharan Africa.

Foreign direct investment

Total recorded inward FDI stock in Africa was broadly unchanged, at USD 938 bn in 2019–21 and USD 948 bn in 2022–24(3). The EU remained the largest recorded investor, but its share fell from 30.8% to 28.5%, as its stock declined from USD 289 bn to USD 270 bn(4).

Gulf investors gained ground: their recorded stock rose by about 40%, from USD 38 bn to USD 54 bn (4.1% to 5.7% of the total), mostly through UAE positions in Mozambique and Mauritius.

India’s share rose by a similar margin, from 3.4% to 5.2%, almost entirely through Mauritius.

The shares of the US (11.2% and 11.5%) and China (4.0% and 4.3%) were broadly stable.

In roughly one in three African economies, a single tracked external actor accounts for more than half of recorded inward FDI stock*. On 2022–24 averages, this is true in 18 of 52 economies: the EU accounts for 12 of these relationships, China for five, and Türkiye for one (Somalia, where recorded stock averages only USD 11 mn).

The EU combined scale with leading position in larger economies – it was the leading listed counterpart in 35 economies representing 86% of measured African GDP.

China was present in almost as many economies but led in only 13, which together accounted for 5.5% of Africa's recorded inward FDI stock and around 12% of measured GDP; many of them are resource-oriented economies.

Its importance therefore lies less in its continental investment share than in its positions within particular hosts.

The Gulf’s footprint was more concentrated; Mozambique, Mauritius and Morocco held 84% of its recorded African position, and the UAE accounted for over four fifths of it.

The US, meanwhile, held substantial positions without often being the leading counterpart: it accounted for 22% of Egypt’s recorded inward stock and 29% of Algeria’s.

Official development finance

Among the bilateral actors and blocs tracked, Europe (MS plus EU institutions) remained the largest source of official finance to Africa, but its lead narrowed. Its net ODA fell by 18.3%, to a total of USD 58.6 billion in 2022–24, with most of the decline coming from EU Institutions.

By contrast, US net ODA rose by 11.0% to a total of USD 45.4 billion, narrowing the gap with Europe.

Saudi Arabia ranked third on net ODA among the tracked actors.

Their broader official-finance portfolios, including OOF (other financial flows), also differed: Europe and the US remained predominantly grant-based, particularily the latter, while loans accounted for 63.2% of Japan’s gross official finance*. Saudi Arabia’s reported ODA disbursements were also largely grant-based.

China operated through a fundamentally different model**. Its USD 47.2 billion in adjusted 2019–23 commitments was overwhelmingly OOF-like rather than ODA and two-thirds was infrastructure-related, and its engagement was geographically concentrated.

Geographic concentration also differs markedly across actors. Europe had the broadest footprint, while the US was more concentrated in East Africa and the Horn and China in West Africa.

The UAE was more concentrated still: around half of its country-coded* African finance went to Central Africa, with 49.3% of its entire portfolio concentrated in Chad.

China’s West Africa concentration was also substantial.

These actor-level shifts also reflected a broader change in financing patterns. Gross finance from sovereign donors plus EU Institutions contracted by 10.0%, while that from other multilateral institutions increased by 19.7%. As a result, total reported gross official finance rose by 2.6%, even as net ODA across all reported providers declined by 3.4%.

Official bilateral lending

China remained by far Africa’s* largest official bilateral creditor, with USD 62.2 billion in claims at the end of 2024, equivalent to 41.8% of bilateral PPG debt.

However, its position in the second period was one of net repayment.

European creditors operated through two distinct channels. EU Member States held USD 30.9 billion, while European institutions held a further USD 13.8 billion, classified as multilateral debt.

Gulf creditors held USD 23.6 billion, concentrated in Egypt and Sudan(5) Russia’s expanding portfolio was even more concentrated, with around 91% of its claims in Egypt, while the US remained a small, largely legacy bilateral creditor.

Creditor portfolios also differed in their terms. Japan and South Korea stood out for highly concessional lending, while around 37% of EU Member States’ bilateral stock was concessional.

Recorded concessional shares were considerably lower for China and Gulf creditors.(6)

This comes against a broader shift in Africa’s external debt. Between 2019 and 2024, it rose by 21% to USD 1,175.5 billion, while its composition shifted sharply towards multilateral creditors. Multilateral PPG claims rose by 49% to USD 264.5 billion, while official bilateral debt remained broadly flat.

Africa’s external financial relationships differ considerably across subregions and financing channels. Europe retained the broadest financial footprint among the actors tracked, leading in recorded FDI and net ODA. Yet its recorded investment share declined, as did its assistance, while the US increased its ODA and narrowed the gap. The investment trends require caution given reporting discontinuities and the considerable weight of financial hubs, particularly Mauritius. FDI positions were also highly concentrated: in roughly one-third of African economies, a single tracked external actor accounted for more than half of recorded inward stock.

Other actors occupied more specialised positions. China’s relatively modest share of continental FDI contrasted with its importance in particular host economies and its dominance in bilateral official lending. It held 41.8% of bilateral public and publicly guaranteed debt at end-2024, although its lending relationship with Africa was characterised by net repayments in 2022–24. Gulf investors, led by the UAE, expanded their recorded positions but remained concentrated in a handful of economies. Saudi Arabia was a major ODA provider, while Japan and South Korea stood out for the concessional terms of their lending.

These relationships are evolving as African borrowing shifts towards multilateral creditors, whose outstanding claims have grown substantially while bilateral debt has remained broadly unchanged. External financial exposure therefore reflects not only the scale of investment, aid and lending, but also their geographical concentration, financing terms and changing direction of financial flows. These differences shape the dependencies and potential leverage associated with external financial relationships, without necessarily translating into political influence.
